# Sound Design: Story Purpose, Layers and Sync

**Purpose:** Teach how to listen to raw sound and to a silent rough cut, how to define the story purpose of sound before choosing effects, how the categories of sound differ, how to layer effects with complementary roles, and how to balance believable sync with emotional timing.

**Applies to:** Every genre — from subtle ambience in documentary to stylised impacts in advertising and drama.

**Prerequisites:** [Story Architecture](../STORY/Story-Architecture.md), [Dialogue and Voice](Dialogue-and-Voice.md).

**Companion chapter:** [Perspective and Silence](Perspective-and-Silence.md) (acoustic perspective, distance, space, silence as design). Level and spectral balance are owned by [Mix Strategy](Mix-Strategy.md); generated effects by [Generation and Layering](../AI-AUDIO/Generation-and-Layering.md).

## 1. Listening before designing

### 1.1 What to listen for in raw sound

| Listen for | What it offers |
|---|---|
| The space's own sound (room tone, ambience) | The truth of the place; continuity material |
| Characteristic events (a generator, a ward bell, a tape gun) | Identity of place and time |
| Sounds that carry story (a cough offscreen, a key in a pocket) | Plot and subtext |
| Perspective clues (near/far, inside/outside) | Raw material for spatial design |
| Unwanted sounds | Problems to remove or embrace |
| Moments of relative quiet | Opportunities for contrast and silence |

### 1.2 What to listen for in a silent rough cut

Watching a rough cut with sound muted — or with only production dialogue — reveals what sound must do:

- **Where is the viewer disoriented?** Sound can orient (an exterior ambience under an interior shot tells us there is a street outside).
- **Where is attention unfocused?** A specific sound can direct the eye.
- **Where does the picture feel weightless?** Foley and effects give materiality.
- **Where does a cut feel abrupt?** A sound bridge or pre-lap may smooth it, or emphasise it.
- **Where is offscreen space unused?** Sound can extend the world beyond the frame.
- **Where does time need to feel longer or shorter?** Sonic density shapes perceived time.

## 2. Purpose before effects

Define what sound must do in each scene before selecting any effect.

| Purpose | Question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Orientation | Where are we, when, what kind of place? | Distant traffic and an evening call to prayer place Project A in a town at dusk |
| Tension | What threat or anticipation should be felt? | The rhythmic squeak of a ceiling fan in an otherwise quiet ward |
| Materiality | What are things made of, how heavy, how real? | The metallic tick of Arc's lid versus a plastic click |
| Scale | How big or small is this? | A low, resonant room tone for a large empty hall |
| Rhythm | What pulse does the scene have? | Footsteps, a ticking clock, machinery |
| Subjective perspective | Whose experience are we hearing? | Muffled sound as Nisha realises the key's meaning |
| Offscreen space | What exists beyond the frame? | A patient coughing offscreen; a television in the next flat |

## 3. Categories of sound

| Category | Definition | Typical source | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Production sound | Recorded on set with picture | Boom, lav, camera mics | Includes dialogue and incidental effects |
| Ambience (backgrounds) | Continuous sound of a place | Location recordings, libraries | Establishes space; covers edits |
| Room tone | The specific quiet of a room as recorded | Recorded on set | Used to fill dialogue gaps; distinct from designed ambience |
| Foley | Sounds performed in sync with picture by a Foley artist (footsteps, cloth, props) | Foley stage or improvised recording | Gives physical presence; essential for international versions |
| Hard effects | Specific, synchronised effects (door slam, car pass) | Libraries, field recording | Literal events |
| Designed effects | Created or heavily processed sounds (whooshes, impacts, sci-fi interfaces, product signatures) | Synthesis, layering, processing | Expressive, often non-literal |
| Non-diegetic sound | Sound not originating in the story world (score, designed transitions) | Music, design | Comments on or shapes experience |
| Diegetic sound | Sound that exists in the story world | All of the above except score | Characters could hear it |

The boundary between diegetic and non-diegetic is a tool: a song playing on a radio in the scene (diegetic) that swells into score (non-diegetic) changes the viewer's relationship to it.

## 4. Sync: believable versus emotional timing

**Believable sync** places sounds exactly where the visible event occurs. Viewers expect it for close, clearly visible actions (a hand putting down a cup, a footstep in close-up).

**Emotional timing** moves sounds slightly — or significantly — for effect:

| Technique | Effect | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Sound slightly early (a frame or two before the visible event) | The event feels "caused" by the sound; makes impacts feel punchier | Arc's ring-light tone placed 1–2 frames before the glow becomes visible |
| Sound arriving before the image (pre-lap) | Anticipation; leads the viewer into the next scene | Birdsong before the dawn shot in Project A |
| Sound outlasting the image | Lingering consequence | The tape gun's screech continuing over the cut to Ravi's face |
| Delayed sound (for distance) | Realism for distant events (sound travels slower than light) | A distant thunderclap after the flash |
| Desynchronised sound | Disorientation, subjectivity | A character in shock hears the world lagging |

A viewer accepts emotional timing as long as the relationship between sound and event remains perceptible, or the desynchronisation is clearly intended.

## 5. Layering with complementary roles

Complex sounds are often built from layers, each with a role:

| Role | What it contributes | Example (an impact) |
|---|---|---|
| Onset (transient) | The attack; defines timing and hardness | A sharp crack or click |
| Body | The main tone; defines material and size | A low thud |
| Detail | Texture; makes it specific | Debris, rattle, a metallic ring |
| Space | Room or environment response | Reflections, reverb |
| Tail | Decay; defines scale and aftermath | A long rumble or ring-out |

**Before EQ, remove redundant layers.** Two layers that both provide "body" fight in the same frequency region, creating mud; equalising them apart is a weaker fix than removing one. Each layer should earn its place by a role no other layer fulfils `[Heuristic]`.

**Sparse versus dense:** dense layering can make a sound huge — and can bury dialogue, music and the performance it accompanies. A small intimate action often needs one well-chosen sound, not five.

## 6. Illustrative, contradictory and motif-based sound

| Approach | Description | Use | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Illustrative | Sound matches what we see | Default for realism | Can feel literal |
| Contradictory (counterpoint) | Sound conflicts with the image — a cheerful jingle over a disaster, silence over an explosion | Irony, shock, subjectivity, comedy | Misread as error |
| Motif | A recurring sound associated with a character, place or idea | Identity; foreshadowing | Overuse |
| Subjective hearing | The soundtrack reflects a character's perception (muffled, focused on one sound, ringing) | Shock, concentration, memory | Confusing if the subjective frame is not clear |
| Comedic counterpoint | Exaggerated or incongruous sounds for humour | Comedy | Undermines serious moments |
| Factual representation | In documentary, sound is evidence of the place | Observational truth | Adding sounds that weren't there misrepresents |

**Factual representation in documentary:** adding library sounds to observational footage (e.g., a siren that wasn't there) can mislead about events. Re-creating ambience that was present but poorly recorded is generally acceptable `[Practice]`; inventing events is not. Where design is used expressively in factual work (a subjective moment), the viewer should be able to recognise it as expression.

### Perception claims, qualified

Sound strongly influences attention and emotion — low-frequency rumbles are often associated with threat, sudden loud sounds with startle, rising pitch with tension. These are tendencies with cultural and individual variation, not guaranteed controls of the viewer's nervous system `[Heuristic]`. Use them as tools, verify with listeners.

## 7. Worked example 1 — A small intimate action (hypothetical, Project D "The Spare Key", 23.976 fps)

**Moment:** Ravi places the key on the table. The insert shot is a close-up: the key meets the wooden surface and slides a centimetre.

**Purpose:** the sound must make the key feel heavy with meaning, but not melodramatic; it is the hinge of the scene.

| Option | Description | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| A. Production sound only | The boom recorded a faint tap | Truthful but almost inaudible — the moment passes |
| B. Foley key on wood, recorded close, with a tiny slide | Clear, intimate, physical | Draws attention to the object; feels real |
| C. Foley plus a designed low "thud" layer and reverb | Big, cinematic | Melodramatic; inconsistent with the room's sound |
| D. Foley, with all other sound (fridge hum) dipped briefly | The key sound stands alone in near-silence | Focus without exaggeration |

**Choice:** **B + D.** A Foley recording of a similar key on a wooden table, close-miked, with the slide. Layer roles: onset (the metal tap) and detail (the slide) — no added body layer, because the key is small. The refrigerator hum is dipped by a few dB for about a second around the key (a subjective focus that most viewers will not notice consciously), then restored.

**Sync:** the tap aligned exactly to the frame of contact — this is a close, visible event, and emotional timing would be noticed.

**Verification:** watch the moment at full speed with the final dialogue; ask a fresh viewer what Ravi put on the table and how it felt. The answer "a key — it felt like a big deal" without "that was loud" is success.

## 8. Worked example 2 — A large stylised impact (hypothetical, festival app launch reel)

**Moment:** at 0:22 in the launch reel (see [Rhythm and Pacing](../EDITING/Rhythm-and-Pacing.md#4-the-intensityunderstanding-map)), after a beat of silence and a phone buzz, the picture cuts to a massive crowd as the music drops.

**Purpose:** a sense of scale and release.

**Layer design:**

| Layer | Role | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Sharp transient (a processed clap) | Onset; defines the exact moment | Library, layered and tuned |
| Sub-frequency swell into a hit | Body; physical weight in venue speakers | Synthesised |
| Crowd roar | Detail and space; identity of the event | Location recording from a previous festival (licensed) |
| Long reverb tail | Tail; scale | Designed |

**Timing:** the onset is placed 1 frame before the cut (at 25 fps, 40 ms) so that the impact feels like it causes the image; the music drop on the next downbeat follows.

**Translation check:** on phone speakers, the sub-frequency body disappears; the transient and crowd carry the impact. On venue systems, the sub layer adds weight. The mix accounts for both (see [Mix Strategy](Mix-Strategy.md)).

**Risk check:** the sudden loud impact after silence must not exceed destination peak limits or startle beyond the brief's intention; true-peak and loudness checked at delivery.

## 9. Counterexample and repair — the over-designed documentary

**Hypothetical counterexample.** An editor "sweetens" Project A's observational night footage with library effects: a heartbeat monitor beep (no monitor was present), dramatic whooshes on cuts, a distant ambulance siren, and a low drone under the emergency. A colleague from the hospital who sees the cut says, "That's not what our ward sounds like." The drone makes the emergency feel like a thriller.

**Diagnosis:** the design invented events (the monitor, the siren) and imposed a genre (thriller) on observational material, breaching the documentary contract (see [Genre and Viewer Contract](../STORY/Genre-and-Viewer-Contract.md)).

**Repair:**
1. Remove invented events (monitor, siren) and the transition whooshes.
2. Rebuild ambience from location recordings: the fan, the generator, distant corridor sounds, recorded on the night or in the same ward with permission.
3. For the emergency, use the production sound — Asha's instructions, footsteps, the trolley — cleaned and balanced so it is intelligible. Remove the drone.
4. Where the production sound is thin (a shot recorded with the camera mic far away), add Foley that matches visible actions (footsteps, the trolley) — these represent what happened.

**Verification:** the hospital colleague reviews the new mix: "Does this sound like the ward?" An audio-only pass of the emergency should convey urgency through people and actions, not drones.

## 10. Micro-decisions

- Cut ambiences on picture cuts only when the location changes; otherwise let them run continuously under cuts.
- When a location changes, consider a short overlap (pre-lap or tail) of ambience to soften or sharpen the transition intentionally.
- Give Foley perspective that matches shot size (closer and drier in close-ups, more room in wides).
- Pitch-shift or vary repeated effects (footsteps, clicks) slightly so they do not sound machine-gunned.
- Keep production effects on separate tracks for international versions.
- Check every designed sound on small speakers; low-frequency-only designs disappear.

## 11. Failure signatures and corrections

| Symptom | Likely cause | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| "It sounds like a library" | Effects with mismatched perspective or character | Choose or record matching sounds; adjust perspective (see [Perspective and Silence](Perspective-and-Silence.md)) |
| "Mud" in busy scenes | Redundant layers in the same role | Remove layers before EQ |
| "It feels fake" in documentary | Invented events or genre-imposed design | Rebuild from truthful sound |
| Sync feels off | Early/late effects on close visible events | Align to contact frames; reserve emotional timing for appropriate moments |
| Impacts vanish on phones | Energy only in sub-bass | Add mid-range onset and detail |
| Repetitive effects | Identical samples repeated | Vary samples and pitch |

## 12. Verification

- **Audio-only narrative pass:** with picture off, can the listener follow the story's spaces and events?
- **Sync check** of visible events at full speed, then frame-step for close-ups.
- **Acoustic continuity** across cuts within a location.
- **Stereotype check:** sounds associated with places or communities (e.g., stock "ethnic" music, generic "Indian street" ambience) should be specific and truthful, not clichés.
- **Overload and masking:** does design obscure dialogue or music? (See [Mix Strategy](Mix-Strategy.md).)
- **Story service:** does each designed sound serve the picture and story, or display technique?
