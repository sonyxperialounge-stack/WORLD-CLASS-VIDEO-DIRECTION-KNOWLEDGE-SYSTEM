# Music Selection and the Function of Score

**Purpose:** Teach how to choose music by what it does to the story — beyond genre and tempo — how score can support, foreshadow, contradict or distance, when no music is stronger, how to escape temp-track attachment, and how licensing scope constrains choices.

**Applies to:** Library music, commissioned score, licensed commercial tracks, generated music (see also [Voice and Music QC](../AI-AUDIO/Voice-and-Music-QC.md)).

**Prerequisites:** [Story Architecture](../STORY/Story-Architecture.md), [Genre and Viewer Contract](../STORY/Genre-and-Viewer-Contract.md).

**Companion chapter:** [Music Editing](Music-Editing.md) (cutting music to picture). Licensing evidence and scope are owned by [Rights, Consent and Disclosure](../OPERATIONS/Rights-Consent-and-Disclosure.md); level relationships with dialogue by [Mix Strategy](Mix-Strategy.md).

## 1. Music does more than set a mood

Music changes how viewers interpret images, how fast time feels, what they expect next and how much they trust what they see. It is one of the most powerful — and most abused — tools in editing. Choosing it by genre ("upbeat corporate") or tempo ("about 120 BPM") ignores most of what it does.

### Properties to evaluate

| Property | What to listen for | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Emotional trajectory | Does the track change over time? Where does it build, resolve, turn? | The piece has a trajectory; the music must be able to follow it |
| Harmonic tension | Unresolved chords, suspensions, minor/major shifts, dissonance | Tension supports anticipation; resolution supports payoff — at the wrong moment they mislead |
| Timbre | Instrument colours: piano, strings, synths, guitars, voices, percussion | Timbre carries associations (intimacy, technology, tradition, luxury) |
| Density | How many things are happening; how busy the arrangement is | Dense music competes with dialogue and image detail |
| Phrasing | Length of musical sentences; where they breathe | Edit points and the pace of picture should fit phrases |
| Arrangement | How instruments enter and leave; stems available | Determines editability and mix space |
| Lyric meaning | What the words say, in every language the audience understands | Lyrics compete with dialogue and may add unintended meaning |
| Cultural associations | Instruments, scales, styles linked to places, religions, eras, communities | Can enrich or stereotype; can signal belonging or appropriation |
| Recognisability | Is the track or its style widely known? | Familiar tracks bring their own memories and associations, and higher licensing costs |
| Room for dialogue | Frequency range and density in the voice region; gaps | Music that occupies the voice range fights dialogue (see [Mix Strategy](Mix-Strategy.md)) |

## 2. Suiting the mood, undermining the story

A track can match the emotional tone of a scene and still damage it:

- **It resolves too early.** A cue that reaches a triumphant resolution before the story's payoff signals "it's over" while the viewer still has questions.
- **It tells the viewer what to feel about real people.** In Project A, a sad piano under Asha's account of a difficult night tells the viewer to pity her — which contradicts the brief's "respect rather than pity".
- **It exaggerates a factual claim.** An urgent, ominous cue under a modest finding ("filters that aren't cleaned work less well") inflates it into a threat.
- **It brings unwanted associations.** A track famous from another film imports that film's meaning.
- **It fights a performance.** Music that is more emotional than the performance makes the performance seem weak; music that is less emotional can make it seem overwrought.

## 3. The functions of score

| Function | What the music does | Example | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Support | Reinforces the emotion already present | Gentle warmth under the founder's family story in the Arc brand film | Redundancy; telling the viewer what they already feel |
| Foreshadow | Signals something coming that the image does not yet show | A low, unresolved tone under Ravi's hand touching his pocket in Project D | Spoiling a surprise if too obvious |
| Contradict (counterpoint) | Creates meaning through opposition | Cheerful music under an image of loss, producing irony or unease | Misread as error or insensitivity |
| Distance | Keeps emotional distance; observational | Sparse, cool textures that do not comment on the image | Can feel cold |
| Pace | Drives momentum or slows time | The kinetic launch reel's percussive track | Imposes pace the content doesn't support |
| Structure | Marks sections; gives a sense of form | A motif returning at each chapter of an explainer | Mechanical predictability |
| Identity | Associates a sound with a brand, character or series | A short signature motif for Arc | Overuse becomes jingle-like |

### Ethical emotional manipulation in factual material

All music in factual work influences interpretation. The ethical question is whether the influence is **proportionate to and supported by the material**. Guidelines `[Heuristic]`:

- Let the material establish an emotion before music supports it; avoid music that *creates* an emotion the material does not contain.
- Avoid scoring real people's distress for maximum effect; consider whether the subject would feel respected.
- Avoid music that implies conclusions the evidence does not support (ominous cues under ambiguous footage of a person).
- Be most cautious in accountability, news-like and health content.

### When no music is stronger

No music is often the strongest choice when:

- The production sound carries the emotion (a ward at night; a family argument).
- The viewer contract is observational truth.
- Silence or ambience after a revelation lets the viewer think.
- Music would compete with dense information.
- A later cue needs contrast to land — the absence of music earlier gives it power.

Project A uses no music until the dawn handover, about three-quarters of the way through the chosen cut (see Section 7).

## 4. Temp-track attachment

Editors often cut to a temporary track (the "temp"). Over weeks of viewing, the team becomes attached to it: its phrasing shapes the edit, its timbre becomes "the sound of the film", and any replacement sounds wrong. When the temp is an unlicensable commercial track, the result is pressure to imitate it.

**How to avoid it** `[Practice]`:

1. **Describe the function in words before choosing a temp:** "Sparse, warm, no percussion, entering after the reversal, with a single sustained harmony change at the dawn handover."
2. **Rotate temps** — try two or three different tracks so no single one becomes attached.
3. **Cut picture first where possible**, then fit music, so the music does not dictate structure.
4. **Brief composers or library searches with the functional description, timings and the picture** — not "make it sound like this famous track".
5. **Do not commission sound-alikes** of protected works. Imitating a specific recognisable track's melody, arrangement and production closely enough to evoke it can create legal and reputational risk; describe function, not a track to copy. (Practical guidance, not legal advice; see [Rights, Consent and Disclosure](../OPERATIONS/Rights-Consent-and-Disclosure.md).)

## 5. Licensing as a production constraint

Music licensing determines what you may use, where, for how long and how you may edit it. The details belong to the rights owner document; the practical questions to ask early:

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Who owns the composition and who owns the recording? | Commercial tracks usually require permission for both (a synchronisation licence for the composition and a master-use licence for the recording); library music often bundles them |
| What media and platforms are covered? | Online organic, paid advertising, broadcast, cinema, events may be separate |
| What territory and term? | Worldwide vs regional; perpetual vs time-limited |
| Is paid advertising included? | Many standard licences exclude paid promotion |
| Are edits permitted? | Some licences restrict cutting, looping, remixing or lyric changes |
| Does a subscription cover this use? | Subscription music services' terms vary; do not assume a subscription clears every use (e.g., client work, paid ads, broadcast) |
| Content identification systems | Platforms may flag licensed library music; know how to clear claims |

Verify the actual licence for the actual use; keep the documentation with the project.

## 6. Worked example 1 — Track evaluation for a persuasive product film (hypothetical, Project B "Arc" 30-second spot)

**Brief stance:** calm confidence; recognition → small surprise → calm confidence; muted-first viewing, but sound-on in pre-roll.

**Functional description:** "Light, warm, rhythmically steady but not driving; one clear phrase change where the reveal happens; room for a soft product sound; ending that resolves cleanly in a short button; no vocals."

**Candidates (library tracks, described):**

| Criterion | Track A: upbeat pop-electronic, 124 BPM, vocal chops | Track B: warm plucked guitar and soft synth pads, 120 BPM, sparse percussion | Track C: minimal piano with ambient textures, 70 BPM |
|---|---|---|---|
| Emotional trajectory | Constant high energy | Gentle build; clear section change at bar 17 | Static, contemplative |
| Harmonic tension | Little | A suspended chord before the chorus — usable for the "hover" moment | Unresolved throughout |
| Timbre associations | Fitness, nightlife | Everyday warmth, craft | Luxury, introspection |
| Density | High | Medium | Low |
| Room for product sound | None | Yes, in the breakdown | Plenty |
| Lyric issues | Vocal chops could be heard as words | None | None |
| Editability | Stems not available | Stems available (drums, bass, guitar, pads) | Stems available |
| Licence | Covers paid social; excludes broadcast | Covers paid social, pre-roll, worldwide, 12 months — confirmed | Same as B |
| Fit with brand stance | Too energetic ("gadget") | Fits | Too melancholic; makes the product feel precious |

**Choice:** Track B, edited to 30 seconds using its stems (see [Music Editing](Music-Editing.md#4-worked-example-1--phrase-level-rearrangement-hypothetical-project-b-arc-30-s-23976-fps)). **Exception:** if the media plan shifted to gym and fitness placements, Track A's energy might suit that audience — but the brand stance would need revisiting, not just the track.

**Verification:** watch with each candidate muted and unmuted; fresh viewers describe the product's personality in three words; the chosen track should elicit words close to "calm", "smart", "everyday".

## 7. Worked example 2 — A reflective documentary scene (hypothetical, Project A "Second Shift")

**Question:** should the film have music at all, and if so, where?

| Option | Effect | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| A. Score throughout (sparse piano) | Smooth, emotional, "documentary" feel | Tells the viewer how to feel about Asha; masks the ward's sonic world |
| B. No music at all | Pure observational truth; ward sounds carry emotion | The ending may feel abrupt without a release |
| C. No music until the dawn handover; a single sparse cue (a few sustained notes) entering as daylight arrives and carrying under the end card | The absence of music makes its arrival meaningful; the cue marks release rather than telling emotion | The cue must not be sentimental |

**Choice:** C, with the cue chosen for restraint: low density, no melody that "cries", a harmonic movement that resolves gently as the generator stops and birdsong begins.

**Rejected alternative (credible):** B. It is the purest option and would be chosen if the cue tested as manipulative. The decision to keep C rests on the test below.

**Verification:** show B and C versions to fresh viewers; ask whether they felt "told what to feel". If C elicits that response more than B, choose B.

## 8. Counterexample and repair — the overly emphatic cue

**Hypothetical counterexample.** A nonprofit film about a village water project ends with a large orchestral cue — swelling strings, choir, timpani — under slow-motion shots of children drinking from a new tap. Funders at a screening are moved; several community members who appear in the film find it embarrassing ("we look like charity cases"). A journalist reviewing the film calls the ending "emotionally coercive".

**Diagnosis.**
- The cue's scale far exceeds the material's (a tap, a few children).
- Slow motion plus swelling music turns real people into symbols of gratitude.
- The emotional peak comes from the music, not from anything people say or do.

**Repair options:**

| Option | Result |
|---|---|
| Replace with a smaller cue (solo instrument) | Better proportion, still telling emotion |
| Remove music; use the real sound of water, children's voices and village ambience; return to normal speed | The scene's joy comes from the people themselves |
| Keep music but delay it to the end card | Release without scoring people |

**Choice:** remove music under the scene; let real sound carry it; bring a modest cue under the end card only. Return the shots to real-time speed.

**Verification:** community members review the new ending; ask funders and community viewers separately how they felt. Success: community members feel represented with dignity; funders still feel moved — now by the people rather than the orchestra.

## 9. Micro-decisions

- Listen to candidate tracks with the dialogue playing, not alone.
- Check lyrics in every language the audience may understand; a lyric in a language the team does not speak may say something inappropriate.
- Note the track's natural ending options early; endings are hardest to fake.
- Prefer tracks with stems for any piece that will have multiple durations.
- Keep the music's entrance motivated: on a cut, on a breath, on a sound, or under existing ambience — not arbitrarily mid-shot.
- Consider how the track behaves on small phone speakers, which reproduce little bass; a track whose identity is in the bass may disappear.

## 10. Failure signatures and corrections

| Symptom | Likely cause | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| "It feels manipulative" | Music creates emotion the material doesn't support | Reduce, delay or remove music |
| "It feels like a stock video" | Generic genre choice | Choose by function; consider no music or commissioned score |
| "Dialogue is hard to hear" | Dense music in the voice range | Choose sparser music; edit stems; mix (see [Mix Strategy](Mix-Strategy.md)) |
| "The ending feels false" | Music resolves; story doesn't | Fix the story payoff; let music follow it |
| "It sounds like another film" | Recognisable track or sound-alike | Choose a different track; brief by function |
| Rights claim after upload | Licence scope not verified | Check licence; clear; replace |

## 11. Verification

- Listening continuity: does the music feel like a coherent piece within the film, not a set of fragments?
- Narrative fit: watch with and without music; what does music add?
- Dialogue space: is every line intelligible with music present?
- Rights: licence covers every version, territory, platform and term.
- Destination behaviour: check on phone speakers and after platform loudness normalisation (see [Audio Delivery](../DELIVERY/Audio-Delivery.md)).
