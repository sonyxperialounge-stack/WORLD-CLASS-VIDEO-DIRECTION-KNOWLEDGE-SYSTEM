# Rights, Consent, Disclosure and Privacy

**Purpose:** Consolidate in one owner document the practical production constraints of permission and honesty — talent, location and music rights; identifiable private information; minors and vulnerable people; archival and third-party material; licence scope; disclosure of reconstruction and generated material; privacy masking; secure handling of assets — and how to record evidence that each permission exists.

**Applies to:** Every project, from the first conversation with a subject to archive.

**Important limitation:** This chapter describes production practice, not legal advice. Laws differ by jurisdiction and change; contracts govern specific relationships. For commercial, broadcast, sensitive or cross-border work, consult qualified legal counsel and the commissioner's standards.

**Ownership:** Other chapters reference this document rather than restating licence or consent rules.

---

## 1. The principle: permission must exist, and you must be able to show it

For every person, place, piece of music, archive clip, artwork, trademark, font and generated asset in a piece, the production should be able to answer: **Do we have permission for this use, and where is the evidence?** "We think it's fine" is not evidence.

## 2. People: releases and consent

### 2.1 What informed consent requires in practice

| Element | Meaning | Practice |
|---|---|---|
| Informed | The person understands what will be filmed, how it will be used, where it will appear, in what languages and formats | Explain in plain language, in their language; give examples ("short clips may appear on social media") |
| Specific | Consent covers the uses described | List uses: the film, excerpts, social versions, translations, promotional use, archive |
| Voluntary | No pressure; refusal has no penalty | Especially important with employees, patients, students, people dependent on the organisation |
| Documented | A signed release or recorded verbal consent, per the commissioner's standards | Store with the project; link to the people log |
| Ongoing | Consent is a relationship; circumstances change | Keep contact; honour reasonable later concerns where possible; know what the release permits if consent is withdrawn |

**Consent formalities are separate from interview conduct.** The interviewer ensures the subject understands (see [Interview Craft](../STORY/Interview-Craft.md#51-rapport-before-recording)); the producer documents consent.

### 2.2 Who needs a release

| Person | Usually needs | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Featured subjects and interviewees | Signed release covering intended uses | Specific to synthetic/AI uses if any are contemplated |
| Performers (actors, voice artists, presenters) | Contract and release; usage terms; buyout or residual terms | Voice cloning or synthetic reuse requires explicit, specific consent |
| Identifiable people in the background | Depends on context, location, jurisdiction and use (editorial vs commercial) | In commercial work, identifiable background people often need releases or masking; in sensitive settings (hospitals, schools), masking is the default |
| Third parties named in testimony | Consider privacy and defamation risk | E.g., Asha's colleague Sunita — ask her consent before including her name and image |

### 2.3 Minors and vulnerable people

Treat as escalation cases:
- Consent from a parent/guardian **and** the child's own agreement appropriate to their age.
- Consider whether filming is necessary at all; whether identification is necessary.
- Medical settings, people in crisis, people with cognitive impairments, people in dependent relationships (employees, patients, students, prisoners): extra care for voluntariness and understanding; involve the institution's policies.
- Avoid content that could expose them to harm later (bullying, discrimination, legal or immigration risk).

## 3. Locations and property

- **Location agreements:** permission to film, times, areas, equipment, crew size; restrictions (e.g., no patients identifiable; no filming of certain rooms).
- **Property and artworks:** recognisable artworks, posters, designs and branded products in frame may raise rights issues in commercial work; editorial contexts differ. Remove, avoid, mask or clear.
- **Trademarks and logos:** commercial work generally avoids unlicensed third-party logos; brand-safety concerns also apply (see the late change in [Production and Handoffs](Production-and-Handoffs.md#8-worked-example-2--multi-specialist-brand-job-with-a-late-cut-change-hypothetical-project-b-23976-fps)).
- **Public places:** rules on filming in public, drones and permits vary by place and change — check locally.

## 4. Music

| Item | What to check |
|---|---|
| Composition rights | Synchronisation licence (or library licence covering composition) |
| Recording rights | Master-use licence (or library licence covering the recording) |
| Media and platforms | Online organic, paid advertising, broadcast, cinema, events, in-store |
| Territory | Worldwide or specific regions |
| Term | Duration of licence; perpetual or limited |
| Edits and alterations | Cutting, looping, remixing, lyric changes |
| Subscription terms | Whether a subscription covers client work, paid ads, broadcast; what happens after cancellation |
| Content identification | How to handle automated claims on platforms |
| Credits | Required credit wording |
| Commissioned score | Ownership, licence back, usage, stems, rights in new versions |

A subscription does not automatically clear every use. Read the actual licence for the actual use (see [Music Selection](../AUDIO/Music-Selection.md#5-licensing-as-a-production-constraint)).

## 5. Archival and third-party material

| Check | Why |
|---|---|
| Provenance and authenticity | Prevent fakes and misattribution |
| Who owns the rights | The uploader is not necessarily the owner |
| Licence scope | Media, territory, term, duration of clip used |
| **Display versus alteration** | A licence to show a clip may not allow cropping, colourising, speed changes, reframing, AI enhancement or 2.5D treatment |
| Embedded rights | Music, people and artworks inside archive clips may need separate clearance |
| Credits | Required wording and placement |
| Context | Using archive in a misleading context can misrepresent people or events even when licensed |

Resolve archive choices before the edit depends on them (see [Footage Assessment](Footage-Assessment.md#5-archival-and-third-party-material)).

## 6. Identifiable private information and privacy masking

Private information includes faces, names, voices, addresses, vehicle number plates, documents, screens, medical information, location indicators and distinctive tattoos or clothing.

| Situation | Practice |
|---|---|
| No consent for an identifiable person | Avoid the shot, crop, or mask robustly (see [Repair and Finishing](../VFX/Repair-and-Finishing.md#5-privacy-masking)) |
| Documents, screens, boards with personal data | Avoid or mask; check reflections |
| Names spoken aloud | Remove from audio; caption appropriately |
| Metadata | Strip location metadata from distributed files where it could expose people |
| Sensitive categories (health, children, location of vulnerable people) | Higher threshold; institutional rules |

Data protection laws in many jurisdictions (for example, the EU's GDPR or India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act) may apply to the personal data in footage and production records. Their specific obligations are outside this library's scope; consult the commissioner's data-protection guidance.

## 7. Disclosure: reconstruction, alteration and generated material

### 7.1 When disclosure is needed

| Situation | Disclosure practice `[Practice]` |
|---|---|
| Reconstruction or re-enactment in documentary | Disclose (title, narration or credit) so viewers do not take it as recorded reality |
| Staged scenes presented in factual contexts | Avoid; if used, disclose |
| Significant digital alteration of factual imagery (removal, insertion, face/voice modification) | Avoid in evidential contexts; if done (e.g., to protect identity), disclose |
| Synthetic voice or generated imagery presented realistically | Disclose according to commissioner standards and platform policies; never present as recorded reality |
| Generated or stylised imagery that is self-evidently illustrative | Disclosure may not be needed; consider audience expectations |
| Advertising with technical substitutions (screen replacement with approved UI) | Must not misrepresent product function; follow advertising standards |

### 7.2 Platform and legal requirements

Several major platforms have introduced requirements or tools for labelling realistic synthetic or altered content, and some jurisdictions (for example, the European Union's AI Act) include transparency obligations for certain AI-generated or manipulated content. Requirements and their scope change; check the current policies of each destination and the applicable law before release, and record the decision.

### 7.3 Disclosure mechanics

- On-screen title or label at the relevant moment.
- Narration ("This scene was reconstructed with actors.").
- End credits.
- Platform disclosure flags or descriptions.
- Internal records (change log, AI-use log) regardless of public disclosure.

## 8. Secure asset handling

| Practice | Why |
|---|---|
| Access control on storage and review links (passwords, expiry, watermarks) | Confidential products, private subjects |
| Encryption for portable drives with sensitive material | Loss and theft |
| Limited distribution of unmasked originals | Privacy |
| Defined retention and deletion | Data minimisation; contracts |
| No uploads to third-party AI or cloud services without permission | Contracts; consent scope (see [Assistance and Control](../AI-WORKFLOWS/Assistance-and-Control.md#4-data-handling-do-not-assume-uploads-are-allowed)) |
| Incident process | Know whom to inform if material leaks |

## 9. Naming conflicts and escalating

When a requested treatment would misrepresent evidence, expose private information, impersonate someone without consent, remove a required accessibility feature, or breach a licence:

1. **Name the conflict** plainly to the requester.
2. **Offer an honest alternative** that meets the underlying goal.
3. **Escalate** to the responsible decision owner (producer, legal, editorial lead, commissioner).
4. **Record** the decision.

Neither silent compliance nor unexplained refusal serves the project.

## 11. Worked example — Consent in a medical setting (hypothetical, Project A)

**Steps taken:**
1. Pre-production meeting with the hospital: agreed filming areas, nights, crew size, no identifiable patients, and the hospital's right to review for patient privacy only.
2. Asha's informed consent: two conversations before filming, in Hindi, explaining the film, social clips, translations and screenings; she chose not to have her surname shown.
3. After the interview, the producer re-confirmed the daughter story's use, as it involves her child.
4. Sunita consented separately.
5. During filming, the crew avoided patients' faces; where unavoidable, shots were logged for masking.
6. Before lock: a privacy review of every shot by the producer and a hospital representative (privacy only), and a consent review against the register.

## 12. Counterexample and repair — the subscription track in a paid campaign

**Hypothetical counterexample.** A small agency uses a track from its music subscription in a client's paid social campaign. Two weeks in, the platform flags the ads; the subscription licence covered the agency's own channels but not client work or paid advertising. The campaign is paused.

**Diagnosis:** licence scope not checked against actual use; assumption that a subscription covers everything.

**Repair:**
1. Pause, assess options: purchase the appropriate licence tier (if offered) for the client and paid use, or replace the track.
2. If replacing, follow [Music Editing](../AUDIO/Music-Editing.md) to fit the new track; re-mix; re-QC; re-deliver.
3. Add a licence-scope check to the brief worksheet and the QC gate.

**Verification:** written licence covering paid use for the client in the relevant territories and term; platform clears the claim.

## 13. Micro-decisions

- Keep the rights register as a living document from day one.
- Photograph signed releases on the day and store securely.
- Ask featured subjects how they want their names spelled and displayed.
- Put licence expiry dates in a calendar shared with the client.
- When in doubt about a background person, ask for consent or don't use the shot.
- Record every disclosure decision, even "not needed", with the reason.

## 14. Failure signatures and corrections

| Symptom | Likely cause | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Takedown or claim | Licence scope gap | Licence correctly or replace |
| Subject objects after release | Consent not informed or specific | Review agreement; respond respectfully; correct where possible |
| Privacy breach | Masking missed in a version or reflection | Remove/replace; incident process |
| Accusation of fakery | Undisclosed reconstruction or alteration | Correct; disclose; policy |
| Archive misuse | Alteration beyond licence | Restore permitted treatment; renegotiate |

## 15. Verification (gate before lock and before delivery)

- Every featured person, location, music track, archive item and generated asset has an entry in the register with evidence and status.
- Disclosure decisions recorded and implemented in every version.
- Privacy review of every version and aspect ratio.
- Licence scope matches the version matrix (platforms, territories, term, paid use).
