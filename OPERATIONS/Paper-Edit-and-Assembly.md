# Paper Edit and Assembly

**Purpose:** Teach how to plan a testimony-driven edit on transcripts before timeline work — marking usable lines verbatim, ordering them into a story, testing the order aloud, and building the first assembly as sound before picture — so that structure and compression decisions are made where they are cheap, visible and honest.

**Applies to:** Interview and testimony-driven work: documentary, brand and advocacy films built on interviews, explainers assembled from contributor speech, any edit where spoken material is the spine. Scripted narrative and fully scripted voice-over do not need this step; their assembly plan already exists as the script.

**Prerequisites:** [Footage Assessment](Footage-Assessment.md), [Interview Craft](../STORY/Interview-Craft.md), [Story Architecture](../STORY/Story-Architecture.md) for the beat map the ordered lines must serve.

**Owns:** The paper edit, the radio cut and the stringout. Line-level compression rules are owned by [Performance Editing](../EDITING/Performance-Editing.md#interview-compression-what-is-permissible); the beat map and restructuring rules by [Story Architecture](../STORY/Story-Architecture.md); transcript accuracy and its failure modes by [Footage Assessment](Footage-Assessment.md#transcript-and-metadata-search--and-their-limits); translation workflow by [Localization](../TEXT-ACCESSIBILITY/Localization.md).

## 1. What a paper edit is — and what it buys

A paper edit is an ordered list of selected transcript passages, each carrying its source timecode, drafted before any timeline assembly. Some teams call it a paper cut. It is built from the selects log and the live log, tested by being read aloud, then executed on the timeline as a radio cut — sound in story order, picture added afterwards.

| What it buys | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Structure decisions become cheap | Reordering two rows takes seconds; re-cutting a sequence takes an afternoon |
| Compression happens with the full answers in view | The honest condition for compressing testimony (see [Performance Editing](../EDITING/Performance-Editing.md#interview-compression-what-is-permissible)) |
| Duration becomes estimable before assembly | Word count and speaking rate predict the radio cut within a workable tolerance |
| The story is tested before picture can seduce | Beautiful material cannot disguise a shapeless story (see [Story Architecture](../STORY/Story-Architecture.md#10-rough-cut-diagnostic-for-beautiful-but-shapeless-material)) |
| Joins are drafted where they can be inspected | A join is two rows side by side, with both full answers one click away |

A paper edit is not a script. It copies the subject's words verbatim — hesitations, qualifications and all — and changes only their order and completeness. The moment wording is "improved" on paper, it stops being an assembly plan and becomes a fabrication plan.

## 2. When to use it, and when it is overhead

| Situation | Paper edit | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Long-form interview material (hours of rushes, one or more speakers) | Essential | The timeline cannot be the first place order is decided |
| Documentary or brand film built on testimony | Essential | The structure lives in the answers, not in the coverage |
| Explainer assembled from contributor speech plus written narration | Useful | Separates gathered speech from written narration before mixing the two confuses both |
| Single short interview, one speaker, a few minutes | Optional | The editor can hold the order in their head; a marked transcript may suffice |
| Scripted voice-over; scripted drama | Not needed | The script is already the assembly plan |

`[Practice]` The dividing line is memory: once the number of candidate lines exceeds what the editor can reliably hold, the paper edit stops being optional.

## 3. Inputs, and their verification state

| Input | Comes from | Carries |
|---|---|---|
| Transcript | The interview recording, via the transcription workflow (see [Assistance and Control](../AI-WORKFLOWS/Assistance-and-Control.md)) | The words — and nothing else: no pauses, no tone, no laughs |
| Selects log | [Footage Assessment](Footage-Assessment.md#3-a-selection-system) | Ratings, defects, alternatives, permissions, context links |
| Live log | [Interview Craft](../STORY/Interview-Craft.md#9-follow-up-listening-and-live-logging) | The pauses, laughs and emotional shifts the transcript cannot record |
| Listening plan | [Interview Craft](../STORY/Interview-Craft.md#2-deriving-questions-from-the-communication-goal) | The few needed answers the piece exists to obtain |
| Beat map draft | [Story Architecture](../STORY/Story-Architecture.md#5-the-beat-map) | The story the ordered lines must serve |

A transcript marked "unverified" (for example an automatic draft) can be used to find and mark lines, but no unverified line may carry story weight into the radio cut without being checked against the audio. The failure modes — misheard negation, invented words in unclear passages, code-switching rendered as nonsense — are catalogued in [Footage Assessment](Footage-Assessment.md#transcript-and-metadata-search--and-their-limits).

## 4. Marking up the transcript

Work through the transcript once, marking candidate lines. A line is a **meaning-unit** — a passage that could stand alone if cut from its neighbours — not necessarily a sentence.

| Column | Content |
|---|---|
| Source TC | Timecode of the line's usable section, matching the selects log |
| Speaker | Who says it |
| Verbatim line | The exact words, including hesitations that carry meaning |
| Context | The question that preceded it; what followed it |
| Warnings | Pause lengths, laughs, tears, self-corrections, overlaps — imported from the live log |
| Rating | Hero / strong / usable, consistent with the selects log |

Three rules protect the marking pass:

- **Copy verbatim, never retype from memory.** A remembered line is a paraphrase, and paraphrases drift toward what the editor wanted to hear.
- **If a line reads better tidied, that is a warning, not an improvement.** The tidied version is what the speaker did not say (see [Performance Editing](../EDITING/Performance-Editing.md#interview-compression-what-is-permissible)).
- **Mark the listening-plan answers first.** Lines that answer the questions the piece exists to ask get found before the merely good lines crowd them out.

## 5. Ordering: from lines to story

Order in three passes, spine first:

1. **Spine:** the listening plan's needed answers, in an order that answers the central question. Test the order with connectives — *this happens, therefore this, but this* — exactly as in the rough-cut diagnostic (see [Story Architecture](../STORY/Story-Architecture.md#10-rough-cut-diagnostic-for-beautiful-but-shapeless-material)). If the honest connective between two lines is "and then", the paper edit is a list, not a story.
2. **Support:** connective tissue, qualifications, the second-best example of a point already made — only where the spine needs them.
3. **Opener and closer, chosen last.** The best opener is often discovered rather than planned; Project A's opening line was the answer to "anything I should have asked?", the last question of the interview.

Draft the open-question ledger alongside the ordering (see [Story Architecture](../STORY/Story-Architecture.md#4-the-open-question-ledger)): every line that opens a question should have a row showing where it is answered.

Estimate duration before building anything: total words of the ordered lines divided by a speaking rate of roughly 2.5–3 words per second `[Heuristic]`, plus the marked pauses. A 6-minute piece at that rate holds roughly 900–1,000 spoken words — a number that disciplines the ordering before a single clip is laid down.

Lines that fall out of the order go to a **reserve list**, not the bin. The reserve is where later repairs come from (see [Section 11](#11-counterexample-and-repair--the-split-that-only-looked-safe)).

| 5 | A001_C028, 10:48:12:00–10:48:40:00 | Asha | Why she continues — "they can't manage alone at night" | Direct answer to the final listening-plan question | Plain delivery, no warning flags | 7 — approach to reflection | Strong |
| 6 | A001_C019, 10:30:50:00–10:31:20:00 | Asha | "I stopped noticing I was tired" (with self-correction) | What tiredness feels like now | ≈4 s pause before the line — protect; generator hum starts mid-answer | 7 — reversal | Hero |

Beats 2, 3, 6 and 8 carry no testimony lines: arrival, the ward, the emergency and the dawn handover are carried by observational picture and sound. The paper edit makes this visible early — a beat with no line needs coverage, and the shot list can be checked against it before the edit begins.

**Where the reserve row went.** Row R did not stay parked. In an early timeline version it was placed after the emergency, where it implied she cried about the patient; the repair restored it to the daughter thread just before the dawn handover (see [Story Architecture](../STORY/Story-Architecture.md#11-counterexample-and-repair--the-misleading-reorder)). The worksheet warning — *do not place under another topic* — is exactly the check that version skipped.

**After the late change.** When the observational emergency is withdrawn ([Project 1, Section 16](../WORKED-PROJECTS/01-Documentary-Second-Shift.md#16-late-change--the-emergency-scene-is-withdrawn)), beat 6 gains a testimony row: Asha's compressed account of an emergency night ("his breathing changed. You learn to hear that…"), joined with a visible jump cut as described in [Performance Editing](../EDITING/Performance-Editing.md#8-worked-example-2--interview-cut-with-missing-coverage-hypothetical-project-a). The paper edit is versioned and the new row is checked against its full answer like any other.

Duration estimate: 6 lines, ≈250 words plus the protected pause — roughly 110–125 seconds of speech against a 6-minute piece; the remaining time is carried by picture, ambience and the end card. The estimate warned the editor that the observational material, not the interview, would set the pace.

Read aloud to a fresh listener, the order should let them answer "why did Dev make Arc?" in one sentence; if it only lets them answer "what does Arc do?", the film has drifted toward the 30-second spot's job.

## 7. Reading the paper edit aloud

Before building anything on the timeline, hand the ordered lines to a reader and listen.

- The reader should be someone who was **not** at the interview: the interviewer hears the original pauses from memory and unconsciously repairs the text while reading.
- After the reading, ask the listener to state the topic and the central question in one sentence. If they cannot, the ordering is not finished.
- The reading reveals what silent reading hides: redundancy between lines, a missing connective, an unanswered hook, an answer that arrives before its question makes sense.

What the reading cannot reveal: delivery. The reader supplies rhythm, emphasis and confidence the subject may not have. A paper edit that reads beautifully can still fall flat as a radio cut — which is why the read-aloud is a filter, not a verdict.

## 8. From paper to timeline: the radio cut and the stringout

**The radio cut** is the paper edit executed as sound: the chosen source clips laid end to end in story order, on interview picture or on black slugs, with handles at every join and a marker at each clip carrying its source TC and worksheet row. It is version 0 of the sequence — name and date it like a cut.

- Watch it at full speed before changing anything.
- Measure the real duration against the [Section 5](#5-ordering-from-lines-to-story) estimate; an error larger than about 20% usually means pauses, overlaps or false starts were not accounted for.
- Compress here first, using the remove/keep table in [Story Architecture](../STORY/Story-Architecture.md#7-compression). Compression at the radio-cut stage is the honest kind: the full answers are one click away, and no cutaway can hide a join that deserves to be seen.

**The stringout** is a different tool: all marked candidate clips strung together in **source order**, not story order, on one track. It exists for scrubbing and retrieval — the editor runs along it to compare alternatives. Small projects may manage with a stringout alone; on larger ones the paper edit is drafted against it, pulling rows into story order.

## 9. Layering picture over the radio cut

Only after the radio cut holds does picture come in: slugs are replaced with selects as coverage, and the interview picture appears where the face matters more than the illustration.

What picture adds: evidence, geography, faces, breathing room. What picture can break: structure holds, but the visual pass introduces its own problems — the same angle twice, a geography reset, an attention collision. Those are picture-pass problems; repair them with picture, and resist reopening the structure because a shot is beautiful (see [Story Architecture](../STORY/Story-Architecture.md#10-rough-cut-diagnostic-for-beautiful-but-shapeless-material)).

The radio cut answered "does the story work". The picture pass answers "does the film work". Keeping the two questions separate is the point of the whole method.

## 10. Multilingual testimony

- **Select and order in the language spoken.** Rhythm, hesitation and emphasis live in the original; a translation flattens them, and a translation's errors reorder into new meanings. The verbatim column of the worksheet stays in the spoken language, with a gloss column added for the team — the gloss is an aid, never the record. In Project A the hook row carries: "बेटी पूछती है कि मैं दिन में क्यों सोती हूँ" (My daughter asks why I sleep in the day).
- **Translate after selection, not before.** Translating the selected lines only, for captions and localized versions, is cheaper and safer than building the paper edit from a full machine translation whose errors then get locked into the structure. The translation workflow itself is owned by [Localization](../TEXT-ACCESSIBILITY/Localization.md).
- **Read aloud in the original.** The listener who states the topic and central question must understand the spoken language; a bilingual team member fills this role, with the gloss column keeping everyone else oriented.

## 11. Counterexample and repair — the split that only looked safe

**Hypothetical counterexample (Project A).** In the first version of this paper edit, the transcript line "The night shift is manageable — if there are two of us on the ward" was split across two story slots: "manageable" anchored a calm beat before the reflection, and the qualification moved to the relationships beat, where staffing belonged. Every row was verbatim and correctly timecoded. The paper edit read well; the radio cut sounded composed.

**Diagnosis.** No words were changed and no sentences joined — which is exactly why nobody applied the compression rules. But splitting a qualified statement across two story slots is itself compression: the claim "manageable" lost the condition that makes it true, and the stakes beat (two nurses, many patients) lost the evidence that made it matter. The page hid it because each row passed a local check; the failure was between rows, and only a check against the full answer catches it. The rules in [Performance Editing](../EDITING/Performance-Editing.md#interview-compression-what-is-permissible) govern what a qualified statement may become — on paper as on the timeline.

**Repair options:**

| Option | Truthfulness | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Keep the full sentence in the Sunita beat; replace the calm beat's line from the reserve list | True | The reflection needs a different line — the reserve list supplies one |
| Play the full sentence once, positioned between the two beats | True | Both beats lean on one line; the ordering must serve it |
| Duplicate the qualification into both rows | True, and visibly so | The calm beat loses some calm — correct, because the calm was conditional |

**Choice:** the full sentence stays intact at the relationships beat (row 4 of the worksheet above), and the approach to the reflection is carried by row 5's plain answer over observational material — which the duration estimate had already predicted would set the pace.

**Verification.** Every worksheet row is checked against its full answer, not just against its neighbours — for joins *and* for splits. The speaker-agreement test applies row by row: *Would the speaker agree this is what they said?* The subject review at lock (see [Interview Craft](../STORY/Interview-Craft.md#16-verification-before-picture-lock)) is the final check, but the point of the paper edit is that this repair cost a table edit, not a re-cut.

## 12. Micro-decisions

- Every row carries its source TC; a row without one cannot be verified and does not go in.
- Copy verbatim; never retype from memory.
- Date and version the paper edit like a cut; note in one line what changed between versions.
- Park fallen lines in the reserve list rather than deleting them — repairs come from the reserve.
- A beat with no line is a coverage instruction, not a gap to fill with narration.
- Read the paper edit aloud with a fresh reader before building the radio cut; afterwards, read the radio cut's markers back against the worksheet.
- Investigate any duration error larger than about 20% before editing — usually unaccounted pauses, not wrong lines.
- In multilingual work the verbatim column stays in the spoken language, and the read-aloud listener must understand it.

## 13. Failure signatures and corrections

| Symptom | Likely cause | Correction | Verification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Radio cut is three times the target duration | Collection, not selection; no spine | Compress against the remove/keep table ([Story Architecture, Section 7](../STORY/Story-Architecture.md#7-compression)) | Duration; listener states the central question |
| Paper edit reads well; radio cut falls flat | The reader supplied a performance the material lacks | Re-rate lines against the live log; pull alternates from the selects log | Full-speed radio cut watch |
| Structure stands only on unverified transcript lines | Verification debt | Check each load-bearing line against the audio before trusting the radio cut | Quote-versus-audio check |
| Subject objects to a compressed line at review | A join or a split changed meaning | Restore the qualification or make the cut visible | Speaker-agreement test against the full answer |
| Fresh viewers confused though the radio cut was clear | The picture pass introduced repetition or a geography break | Repair picture; keep structure | Attention map; fresh-viewer test |

## 14. Verification

- Every load-bearing line is checked against the audio, not only against the transcript.
- The read-aloud uses a fresh reader and a fresh listener; the listener states the topic and the central question.
- Every row is checked against its full answer — for what was joined, and for what was split away.
- The radio cut is watched at full speed, and its measured duration is recorded against the estimate.
- Compressed sections are reviewed with the subject where agreed (see [Interview Craft](../STORY/Interview-Craft.md#16-verification-before-picture-lock), [Review and Revisions](Review-and-Revisions.md)).
