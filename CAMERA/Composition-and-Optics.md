# Camera Composition, Optics and Coverage

**Purpose:** Teach the first camera question — what must the audience notice, understand or feel, and from whose perspective — and how frame size, angle, height, distance, lens, focus, depth and layering answer it; correct common optical myths; and plan coverage, alternate framings and multi-format acquisition for the edit.

**Applies to:** Every shoot, from phone documentaries to controlled studio production.

**Prerequisites:** [Creative Brief](../FOUNDATIONS/Creative-Brief.md), [Story Architecture](../STORY/Story-Architecture.md).

**Companion chapters:** [Capture Decisions](Capture-Decisions.md) (frame rate, shutter, exposure, flicker, devices); [Motivated Movement](Motivated-Movement.md). Lighting in [Reading and Designing Light](../LIGHTING/Reading-and-Designing-Light.md).

---

## 1. The first camera question

Before choosing a lens or a position, answer:

1. **What must the audience notice?** (A face, a hand action, a product detail, a relationship between two people, a place.)
2. **What must they understand?** (Geography, process, cause and effect, who is speaking to whom.)
3. **What should they feel?** (Intimacy, distance, tension, admiration, unease.)
4. **From whose perspective?** (A neutral observer, a participant, a character's point of view, an admirer of an object, an interrogator.)

Every compositional choice is an answer to these questions. A "beautiful shot" that answers none of them is decoration.

## 2. The variables of a shot

| Variable | What it controls | Typical effect |
|---|---|---|
| Frame size (wide → close) | How much of the subject and context is seen | Wide: context, relationship to place; close: emotion, detail |
| Angle (horizontal) | Front, 3/4, profile, back | Frontal: direct, confronting; 3/4: natural, dimensional; profile: observational, detached |
| Height | Eye level, low, high | Eye level: neutral equality; low: stature, threat; high: vulnerability, overview — all context-dependent |
| Distance (camera to subject) | Perspective relationships, intimacy, intrusion | Closer camera exaggerates depth and facial proportions; farther flattens apparent depth (at matched framing) |
| Lens field of view | How much of the scene the frame includes from a given position | Wide FOV includes more; narrow FOV isolates |
| Focus and depth of field | What is sharp; how quickly sharpness falls off | Shallow: isolation, emphasis; deep: context, relationships |
| Negative space | Empty area around the subject | Look room, loneliness, anticipation, room for text |
| Layers | Foreground, midground, background | Depth, context, framing |
| Subject–background relationship | Separation by light, colour, focus, distance | Clarity and meaning (a background can comment on the subject) |

## 3. Correcting optical myths

### 3.1 Focal length changes field of view; camera position changes perspective

A common belief is that "long lenses compress faces" and "wide lenses distort them." More precisely:

- **Perspective** — the relative sizes and spacing of near and far objects — is determined by the **camera's position** relative to the subjects.
- **Focal length** (with sensor size) determines **field of view** — how much of that perspective is included in the frame.

If you photograph a face from 50 cm away, the nose is much closer to the camera than the ears, so it appears relatively larger — regardless of lens. A wide lens is usually *why* you are that close (to fill the frame), so wide lenses get blamed. From 3 metres away, the nose-to-ear distance is small relative to the camera distance, so proportions flatten; a longer lens is used to fill the frame from there.

**"Compression"** — the apparent flattening of distance between foreground and background — therefore requires **matching the framing through distance**: compare a wide lens close to the subject with a long lens far from the subject, both framing the subject the same size. The background appears larger and closer in the long-lens shot because the camera is farther away, not because the lens squeezes space.

### 3.2 Sensor size and crop

The same focal length gives different fields of view on different sensor sizes. A 50 mm lens on a smaller sensor frames more tightly than on a larger one. When comparing lenses across cameras, compare **field of view** (or "equivalent focal length" relative to a reference format), and state the sensor/format. Depth of field at matched framing and matched f-number also differs with format.

### 3.3 No magical cinematic focal lengths

There is no focal length that makes images "cinematic". Many celebrated films use wide lenses close to actors; many use long lenses. What matters is the relationship between position, field of view, depth, movement and story. Recipes like "always shoot interviews at 85 mm" are habits that may suit one context and fail another.

## 4. Depth cues and how to create them cheaply

Viewers perceive depth from several cues; expensive shallow depth of field is only one.

| Depth cue | How to create it | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Overlap | Place objects partly in front of others | Free (blocking) |
| Layering | Foreground element, subject, background | Free (position) |
| Relative size | Similar objects at different distances | Free |
| Light falloff | Subject brighter than background, or alternating light/dark layers | Low (placement; see [Practical Setups](../LIGHTING/Practical-Setups.md)) |
| Atmospheric perspective | Haze or contrast loss with distance | Low to moderate (natural haze, haze machines with safety care) |
| Colour separation | Warm subject against cool background or vice versa | Low (placement, practicals, white balance) |
| Depth of field | Wider aperture, longer focal length, closer focus, larger format | Moderate (lens/sensor); focus risk |
| Motion parallax | Camera movement causes near objects to move faster than far | Low with a slider or careful handheld |

**Practice** `[Heuristic]`: move the subject away from the background and add a foreground element before reaching for a faster lens. A subject 3 metres from a wall reads more three-dimensional than one 30 cm from it, even at the same aperture.

## 5. Composition for the edit

The camera serves the edit. Shots that cannot be cut together are wasted, however beautiful.

| Edit need | Acquisition response |
|---|---|
| Continuity across angles | Consistent eyelines, screen direction and action (see [Continuity and Attention](../EDITING/Continuity-and-Attention.md)) |
| Cutting within interviews | Second angle or alternate framing; cutaways tied to content |
| Performance choices | Coverage of listeners and reactions, not just speakers |
| Visual effects | Clean plates (the same shot without subjects), tracking markers, lens data (see [Compositing and Integration](../VFX/Compositing-and-Integration.md)) |
| Transitions | Handles: rolling before action and after its completion |
| Pacing flexibility | Shots with clear in and out points; moves that start and end on stable frames |

### Shot lists based on story beats, not shot sizes

A weak shot list is a catalogue: "wide, medium, close, insert, cutaway." A strong shot list derives shots from beats:

| Beat (Project D) | What the audience must notice/understand/feel | Shots |
|---|---|---|
| Nisha arrives; Ravi already there | Surprise; tension; the flat as a place of memory | Wide from the doorway (her POV entering); Ravi's reaction single |
| The argument | Their positions; escalating conflict; the photo frame | Matched singles at similar sizes; OTS for confrontation; insert of the photo |
| Ravi's hidden reason | Something in his pocket | Insert of his hand; a single where his hand is visible in frame |
| The key reveal | The key's weight; Nisha's re-evaluation | Insert of the key landing; Nisha single (tight); a wide to re-establish |
| Release | The distance between them closing — or not | A held two-shot wide |

## 6. Multi-format acquisition: horizontal and vertical

When both 16:9 and 9:16 versions are planned, acquisition must plan for both. Options:

| Approach | How | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shoot 16:9 and crop 9:16 | Frame wide enough to extract vertical; high-resolution capture | Single camera; cheap | Vertical crops lose context; composition compromises both versions |
| "Protect" framing | Compose 16:9 with key action within a central vertical-safe zone | Simple | Centre-heavy framing weakens the horizontal version's composition |
| Second camera framed vertically | A dedicated camera (or phone) shooting 9:16 | Best vertical compositions | Extra operator/equipment; continuity |
| Alternate takes | Re-shoot key moments for vertical | Strong results | Time; performance repetition |
| Shoot for vertical first | When vertical is primary | Optimised for primary platform | Horizontal versions suffer |

**Why indiscriminate centre framing weakens all formats:** placing every subject in the centre to "protect" vertical makes the horizontal frame static and symmetrical without reason, removes look room and relationships, and still often fails vertically (because vertical needs different headroom and space for interface overlays). Plan which shots need vertical protection and which can be recomposed or replaced.

## 7. Worked example 1 — An intimate interview (hypothetical, Project A "Second Shift")

**Intent:** close, respectful, unhurried; the viewer feels they are sitting with Asha, not interrogating her.

| Decision | Choice | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Position | Camera about 1.5 m from Asha, at her seated eye height | Eye-level equality; distance close enough for intimacy, not intrusive |
| Angle | 3/4, with her eyeline just off the lens toward the interviewer | Observed conversation; natural |
| Lens/FOV | A moderately tight field of view (roughly a "normal-to-short-tele" equivalent) framing from mid-chest | Avoids the facial exaggeration of very close wide shooting |
| Depth | Asha about 2.5 m from the back wall; background softly out of focus but recognisable (a shelf, a calendar) | Context and separation |
| Negative space | Look room on the side of her eyeline; headroom moderate | Gives her look somewhere to go |
| B-cam | Phone at a wider 3/4 from the other side | Cutting angle; context |
| Vertical planning | A-cam framing leaves enough width to extract a 9:16 with her face offset and look room preserved for the social excerpt | See [Continuity and Attention](../EDITING/Continuity-and-Attention.md#11-worked-example--interview-with-missing-coverage-and-later-vertical-extraction-hypothetical-project-a) |
| Background meaning | A shelf with a framed photo of nurses — truthful, quietly meaningful | Subject–background relationship |

**Verification on set:** check the frame on a monitor at the size it will be viewed; confirm eyeline consistency between answers; confirm the 9:16 extraction area contains her face and some look room; record a few seconds of the empty frame (clean plate) in case cleanup is needed.

## 8. Worked example 2 — A product demonstration (hypothetical, Project B "Arc")

**Intent:** the viewer sees the lid ring respond to drinking, clearly, and perceives the product as precisely made.

| Decision | Choice | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Key shot | Macro of the lid ring as the bottle tilts, camera slightly above the lid, ring occupying a substantial part of the frame | Legibility on phones |
| Angle | Low 3/4 on the product in the hand for the drinking shot; a hand shown naturally | Human scale; avoids "product floating in space" |
| Depth | Shallow enough to isolate the ring, deep enough to keep the full ring sharp (focus tests at the planned aperture) | Partial sharpness would hide the progress segments |
| Reflections | Brushed steel reflects the environment; large soft white and black cards placed to shape highlight lines | Material legibility (see [Practical Setups](../LIGHTING/Practical-Setups.md)) |
| Layers | Background kitchen softly visible, warm-neutral | Everyday context |
| Vertical | Separate vertical framing for the macro and the hand shot | Ring and hand must both fit 9:16 |
| Coverage | Real-time and high-frame-rate versions of the pour and drink; clean plate of the phone screen for UI replacement | Retiming and VFX (see [Capture Decisions](Capture-Decisions.md), [Compositing and Integration](../VFX/Compositing-and-Integration.md)) |

## 9. Counterexample and repair — sharp but emotionally detached

**Hypothetical counterexample.** In a documentary about a retired teacher, the camera operator frames her from across the room with a long lens: perfectly sharp, beautifully exposed, the subject small in a well-composed interior. The editor notes that every interview shot feels "like surveillance". Viewers describe the teacher as "distant" and "hard to read".

**Diagnosis:** the camera's distance and profile angle created observational detachment; the long lens isolated her without closeness; her expressions were too small in the frame on phones.

**Repair (next interview session, or with available footage):**
- Move the camera closer (about 1.2–1.5 m), at eye height, 3/4 angle with the eyeline just off-lens.
- Use a field of view that frames her from chest up, so expressions read on small screens.
- Keep a contextual wide angle as a B-cam for occasional context.
- With existing footage only: use the highest-resolution source to punch in moderately on key answers, accepting some quality loss; rely on her voice and cutaways of her hands and objects to create intimacy.

**Verification:** compare reactions of fresh viewers to the original and revised framings; ask, "How well do you feel you know her?"

## 10. Micro-decisions

- Place the camera lens at the subject's eye height for neutral interviews; a few centimetres higher or lower changes perceived status.
- Check headroom relative to the *final* aspect ratio and platform overlays, not just the camera frame.
- Avoid tangents (an edge of the frame or a background line touching the subject's head).
- Watch for objects "growing out" of heads in the background.
- Keep consistent shot sizes for matched singles in a dialogue scene; mismatched sizes imply unequal importance.
- In product macros, check focus at the exact point of the feature, not the nearest edge.

## 11. Failure signatures and corrections

| Symptom | Likely cause | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| "Flat" images | Subject against a wall; no layers; even light | Move subject from background; add foreground; light falloff |
| Interviews feel distant | Camera far; profile angle; small subject | Closer, eye level, 3/4, larger in frame |
| Distorted faces | Camera too close (with a wide lens) | Move back; use narrower FOV |
| Vertical version fails | No vertical planning | Plan protect zones, second camera or alternate takes |
| Shots don't cut | Inconsistent eyelines, sizes, directions | Plan coverage by beats; continuity checks |
| Product unreadable on phones | Feature too small or partly out of focus | Tighter framing; focus at the feature; depth tests |

## 12. Verification before leaving the set

- **Required information:** every beat's must-see element is captured clearly.
- **Eye direction:** eyelines consistent and correct for the edit.
- **Exposure and focus:** checked at full magnification for critical shots (see [Capture Decisions](Capture-Decisions.md)).
- **Format-safe framing:** every version's crop zone contains what it needs.
- **Coverage:** the edit has options for each beat — reactions, cutaways, clean plates, handles.
