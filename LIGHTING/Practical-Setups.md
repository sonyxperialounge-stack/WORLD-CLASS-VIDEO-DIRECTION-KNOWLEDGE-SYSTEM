# Practical Lighting Setups

**Purpose:** Compare the main lighting approaches — motivated single source, window with negative fill, conventional key/fill/back, practical-driven and deliberately stylised — and work through a window-lit interview, a night scene with practical fixtures and a reflective product, with diagnoses of common failures.

**Applies to:** Interviews, dramatic scenes, product and demonstration shoots, with any budget.

**Prerequisites:** [Reading and Designing Light](Reading-and-Designing-Light.md).

---

## 1. Five approaches compared

| Approach | Description | Strength | Weakness | Choose when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Motivated single source | One dominant source, justified by the scene (window, lamp), plus control (bounce, negative fill, flags) | Natural; coherent shadows; fast | Less control over fill and separation; depends on location | Documentary, naturalistic drama, intimate interviews |
| Window with negative fill | Window as key; black on the shadow side to add contrast | Beautiful modelling cheaply | Changes with weather and time; window may be in the wrong place | Day interiors; small crews |
| Conventional key / fill / backlight (three-point) | Key for main modelling; fill to control contrast; backlight for separation | Repeatable; teachable; separates subject from background | Generic, unmotivated if applied without reading the space; backlight can look artificial | Inexperienced crews; multi-interview corporate series needing consistency; studios with no natural light |
| Practical-driven | Visible lamps, screens and signs light the scene (often with hidden supplementary sources motivated by them) | Atmospheric; believable night interiors; actors can move freely | Practicals are dim and colourful; flicker risk; exposure demands | Night interiors, bars, homes, period pieces |
| Deliberately stylised | Coloured, hard, graphic or theatrical light not tied to realistic sources | Expressive; brand identity; music videos | Can feel gimmicky; skin rendering challenges | Music videos, fashion, stylised brand films, dream sequences |

### When three-point lighting helps — and when it hurts

**Helps:**
- A crew with little experience needs a reliable, teachable starting structure.
- A series of corporate interviews must look consistent across days, rooms and operators.
- A studio has no motivated sources at all.

**Hurts:**
- In a location with strong motivated light, a generic key/fill/back ignores it and produces an image that looks "lit" — the backlight in particular often has no plausible source.
- In observational documentary, the setup signals production rather than presence.
- Applied identically to every subject, it ignores faces' different structures and skin tones.

A practical compromise `[Heuristic]`: think of three *functions* (main modelling, contrast control, separation) rather than three fixtures. A window can be the key; negative fill can be the "fill" (controlling contrast downward); a practical lamp in the background can provide separation.

## 2. Worked example 1 — Window-lit interview (hypothetical, Project A "Second Shift")

**Assumptions:** room about 4 × 5 m; one window about 1.5 m wide on the left wall from the camera's view; overcast sky; overheads switched off; camera at Asha's eye height; 25 fps, 1/50 s; log profile.

**Top-down diagram** (not to scale; included because positions relative to the window determine the result):

```
              BACK WALL (shelf, calendar) — kept about 1 stop or more below the face
         ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
         │                                                    │ W
  W      │                    [ASHA]                          │ H
  I      │   window light ►    faces toward the               │ I
  N      │                     interviewer (left)    [BLACK   │ T
  D      │                                           FABRIC]  │ E
  O      │        [INTERVIEWER]                      negative │
  W      │         beside lens                       fill     │ W
         │                                                    │ A
         │                    [A-CAM]                         │ L
         │                                  [B-CAM phone,     │ L
         │                                   wider 3/4]       │
         └────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
  Left wall: window (key). Right wall: white (would bounce fill; blocked by the black fabric).
```

**Setup decisions:**

| Element | Choice | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Key | Window, large and soft | Motivated, soft modelling |
| Subject position | About 1.5–2 m from the window, facing roughly toward it at an angle; camera placed for short-side lighting | Modelling with soft falloff |
| Negative fill | Black fabric on a stand on the shadow side, blocking bounce from the white wall | Restores contrast that the white wall's bounce would erase |
| Background | Asha about 2.5 m from the back wall; a card flags window light off the shelf if it reads too bright | Separation by brightness |
| Fill | None added; adjust negative fill distance to taste | Contrast set by removing light, not adding |
| Backlight | None; separation from the darker background is sufficient | Avoid an unmotivated rim |
| Practicals | None in frame | Avoid mixed colour |
| Catchlight | The window visible as a soft catchlight in her eyes | Life; confirms source |

**Changes over time:** clouds will vary exposure; the director monitors and pauses for large changes; the B-cam provides cutaways (as needed later at the light dip). If the sun breaks through and strikes the window directly, a diffusion sheet (or the curtain, if thin and neutral) is used.

**Low-budget note:** no fixtures were used. The improvements came from switching off overheads, positioning, and one black fabric.

**Verification:** test at the shooting exposure, through the actual monitoring LUT; ask Asha to lean forward and back to see the light change; recheck after an hour.

## 3. Worked example 2 — Night interior with practical fixtures (hypothetical, Project D "The Spare Key", 23.976 fps, 1/50 s)

**Intent:** the flat at night feels lived-in and melancholy; pools of warm lamplight separate the siblings, with darkness between them as emotional distance (see [Reference Analysis](../FOUNDATIONS/Reference-Analysis.md#61-labelled-moodboards)); faces must remain readable on phones at festival-streaming sizes.

**Sources:**

| Source | Role | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Table lamp near the sofa (in frame) | Motivates Nisha's key | Warm; the bulb dimmed and shielded on the camera side to avoid clipping |
| Floor lamp near the window (in frame) | Motivates Ravi's key | Warm |
| Streetlight through the window | Cool background accent and edge light | Simulated with a fixture outside the window, gelled slightly cool |
| Hidden soft source (a small LED panel with diffusion) near the table lamp, off-camera | Supplements Nisha's key at a usable level | Placed along the lamp's direction so shadows are consistent |
| Hidden source near the floor lamp | Supplements Ravi | Same principle |
| Ceiling light | Off | It would flatten everything |

**Colour:** camera balanced so that faces under the warm practicals read warm but not orange — between tungsten and daylight balance (a choice tested on camera with both actors' skin tones). The streetlight reads cool by contrast.

**Flicker:** shutter 1/50 s to avoid mains flicker; the hidden LED panels are flicker-free fixtures; the practical lamps were tested at the chosen dimmer levels (see [Capture Decisions](../CAMERA/Capture-Decisions.md#9-worked-example-1--led-practical-flicker-hypothetical-project-d-23976-fps-in-a-50-hz-region)).

**Exposure:** faces placed in a comfortable range above noise with the log curve; the lamp shades allowed to be bright but not clipped harshly; the darkness between the pools is dark but not crushed in the recording (the colorist decides how dark it finally becomes).

**Continuity:** lamp positions and dimmer levels marked; photographs taken; the fixture outside the window repositioned consistently for reverse angles.

**Verification:** test with actors walking between pools; check faces at each blocking mark; view on a phone-sized monitor to confirm faces read.

## 4. Diagnoses and repairs

| Symptom | Likely cause | Repair on set | Repair in post (limited) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flatness | Frontal or overhead light; heavy bounce from walls | Side key; negative fill; switch off overheads | Local contrast shaping; soft windows to add modelling — cannot add real shadows |
| Distracting background brightness | Background closer to the source; bright window or wall behind | Flag; move subject forward; curtains; ND on window | Darken background with a mask; risk of halos around hair |
| Inconsistent skin between shots | Changing daylight; mixed sources; different camera settings | Monitor and pause; unify sources; lock white balance | Shot matching (see [Correction and Shot Matching](../COLOR/Correction-and-Shot-Matching.md)) |
| Implausible source direction | Key placed against visible motivation | Re-position key or camera | Limited relighting; shadows remain wrong |
| Shiny skin hot spots | Hard key; sweat; heat | Soften source; powder (with consent); cool the room | Highlight reduction; skin smoothing risks waxiness |
| Glasses reflections | Source reflected in lenses | Raise the source; tilt glasses slightly; move camera | Difficult; often impossible to remove cleanly |
| Product looks grey/flat | Reflects uniform white surroundings | Black flags and gradient sources | Contrast and local shaping — limited |

## 5. Reflective product (hypothetical, Project B "Arc")

**Problem:** brushed steel reflects the environment; the glossy lid ring reflects sources; the ring's own glow must read.

| Decision | Choice | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Body highlights | Two tall, narrow soft sources (strip softboxes or diffusion frames) at the sides, creating long gradient highlights along the bottle | Communicates cylindrical metal form |
| Edge definition | Black flags just outside frame on both sides | Dark reflections define the edges |
| Top | A black card above the lid | Keeps the lid area dark so the ring's glow reads |
| Background | Warm-neutral kitchen set, softly lit, separated by distance | Context without stray reflections |
| Hand | Soft light on the hand from the front-side | Skin rendering; avoid hard shadows on the product |
| Ring glow | Exposure set so the ring is visibly brighter than surroundings without clipping its colour | Proof point legibility |
| Fingerprints | Gloves for handling between takes; cleaning; fresh product units | Shiny surfaces reveal every smudge |

**Verification:** tethered or monitored test shots at the exact exposure; check the ring's colour channels on a waveform/parade for clipping; view on a phone.

## 6. Counterexample and repair — the "corporate" three-point in a home

**Hypothetical counterexample.** For a short documentary about a grandmother's cooking, a corporate crew sets up their standard three-point kit in her kitchen: large soft key frontal-left, fill frontal-right, a strong backlight from behind her head. The result: a flatly lit face, an obvious rim light with no source (the kitchen window is to her side), and a background lit evenly. The kitchen's warm, low evening light — the reason the director chose the time — is gone. The grandmother feels uneasy under the lights.

**Diagnosis:** the setup ignored the location's light and the documentary contract; the backlight is unmotivated; the fill removed modelling; the equipment changed the subject's comfort.

**Repair:**
1. Switch off the kit. Re-read the kitchen at the chosen time: low sun through the side window, a warm bulb above the stove.
2. Use the window as key; add negative fill on the room side if needed.
3. If the window light fades, supplement with a small soft source placed *in the window's direction*.
4. Let the background fall off naturally; the stove bulb remains as a practical accent.
5. Fewer fixtures, smaller crew presence.

**Verification:** the director and grandmother compare a test shot with the room as it normally looks: "Does this feel like your kitchen?"

## 7. Micro-decisions

- Keep fixtures out of the subject's direct eyeline; glare makes people squint and tense.
- Mark floor positions for lamps and stands with tape; photograph each setup.
- Use the dimmest effective level; heat and glare affect performance.
- When supplementing practicals, keep the added source's direction consistent with the practical's position.
- In mixed light, decide which source "owns" skin, and keep others off faces.
- Check reflections in eyes, glasses, products and windows from the camera position before rolling.

## 8. Verification

- At intended camera settings and monitoring.
- Through subject movement across marks.
- Over the shooting period (daylight changes, dimmers, practical heat).
- On a small screen for legibility of faces and products.
