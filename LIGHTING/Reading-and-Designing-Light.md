# Reading and Designing Light

**Purpose:** Teach how to read a location's existing light before touching a fixture, how the qualities of light (direction, size, softness, falloff, colour) shape visibility, texture, faces, space and emotion, and how lighting connects to blocking, exposure, lenses, continuity, safety and what post-production can and cannot change.

**Applies to:** Every shoot with any control over position, time or light — including documentaries with no lights at all.

**Prerequisites:** [Composition and Optics](../CAMERA/Composition-and-Optics.md), [Capture Decisions](../CAMERA/Capture-Decisions.md).

**Companion chapter:** [Practical Setups](Practical-Setups.md) compares setups and works through window-lit interviews, night practicals and reflective products. Colour correction and grading are in [Correction and Shot Matching](../COLOR/Correction-and-Shot-Matching.md) and [Creative Grading](../COLOR/Creative-Grading.md).

## 1. Read the location before touching a fixture

Every location already has light — and a story about light. An expert's first act is observation, ideally at the time of day the shoot will happen.

| Observe | Questions | What it suggests |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | Where does the dominant light come from? Window, ceiling, practical lamps, open sky? | The motivated key direction; which side of faces will be lit |
| Intensity | How bright, and how does it vary across the space? | Exposure; where subjects can stand |
| Softness | Large source (overcast window) or small (bare bulb, direct sun)? | Shadow edge quality; texture |
| Shadow shape | Where do shadows fall on faces and walls? | Facial modelling; mood |
| Practical sources | Lamps, screens, signs, candles | Motivation for added light; colour accents; flicker risk |
| Windows | Size, direction, what's outside (sky, buildings, trees), curtains | Main source by day; changes over time |
| Ambient level | How much fill comes from the room bouncing light back | Contrast ratio without intervention |
| Colour relationships | Daylight vs tungsten vs fluorescent vs LED; green or magenta tints | White-balance strategy; mixed-colour decisions |
| Reflective surfaces | Glass, polished floors, white walls, screens, the product itself | Unwanted reflections; free bounce |
| Subject movement | Will people move through light and shadow? | Consistency; exposure range |
| Change over time | Sun moving, clouds, dusk, practicals switched on and off | Scheduling; continuity risk |

**The output of reading is a story and attention plan**, not a lighting plot: *"Asha should be lit from the window side, which is also the side of her eyeline toward the interviewer; the back wall should stay a stop or more darker than her face so the eye goes to her; the window's light will drop in late afternoon, so we shoot the most important questions before 15:00."*

## 2. The qualities of light

### 2.1 Hard and soft

Softness depends on the **apparent size of the source relative to the subject** — how large it looks from the subject's position — not on the fixture's label.

| Quality | Cause | Effect on faces and objects | Communicates (context-dependent) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hard | Small apparent source (bare bulb, direct sun, distant fixture) | Sharp shadow edges; strong texture; specular highlights | Clarity, heat, harshness, drama, glamour, interrogation |
| Soft | Large apparent source (overcast sky, big window, diffusion close to subject, bounce) | Gradual shadow transitions; less texture | Gentleness, intimacy, calm, naturalism |

A large source placed far away becomes smaller in apparent size and harder; the same source close becomes softer. Diffusion close to the subject softens more than diffusion close to the lamp.

**Neither is inherently beautiful.** Soft light can flatten a face and erase character; hard light can reveal texture and carve strong features. "Soft is flattering" is a generalisation that fails for faces that benefit from structure, for scenes that need sun, and for stories that need edge.

### 2.2 Falloff

Light intensity decreases with distance from the source (for a small source, approximately with the square of distance). Practical consequence: **a source close to the subject falls off quickly** — the subject is bright, the background much darker. A source far away lights subject and background more evenly.

| Want | Do |
|---|---|
| Subject bright, background darker (separation, mood) | Bring the source closer to the subject; move the subject away from the background |
| Even light across a group or space | Move the source farther away (using more power) or use a large, distant source |

### 2.3 Shaping tools

| Tool | What it does | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Diffusion | Spreads light, increasing apparent source size | Softening |
| Bounce | Reflects light from a surface (white card, wall, reflector), creating a large, soft source | Fill; soft key from a window bounce |
| Negative fill | A dark surface (black fabric, flag) that absorbs light and prevents bounce | Increasing contrast on the shadow side; restoring modelling |
| Flags | Block light from areas | Keep light off backgrounds, lenses, reflective objects |
| Spill control (grids, barn doors, snoots) | Limit spread | Precision; separate areas |
| Dimmers / ND gels on windows | Reduce intensity | Balance windows with interiors |

### 2.4 Specular behaviour

Shiny surfaces (skin with sweat, glasses, metal, glass, screens) reflect the *shape* of light sources. The highlight's shape and position communicate material: a long soft gradient on a brushed-steel bottle reads as "premium metal"; a hard pinpoint reads as "small glossy object". Managing speculars means controlling the size, shape and position of sources and of what surrounds the object (see [Practical Setups](Practical-Setups.md#5-reflective-product-hypothetical-project-b-arc)).

### 2.5 Background separation

Viewers find the subject more easily when it differs from the background in brightness, colour, focus or texture. Options: darker background (flag light off it; move subject away), a backlight or edge light (motivated, ideally), a colour contrast (warm subject, cooler background), depth of field.

## 3. Light as communication

| Light shapes | How |
|---|---|
| Visibility | What can be seen at all — and what is hidden |
| Texture | Raking (side) light reveals surface texture; frontal light hides it |
| Facial structure | Direction and softness model cheekbones, eyes, jaw; frontal flat light flattens |
| Space | Pools of light and darkness define depth and areas |
| Emotion | Contrast, colour and darkness create mood — by convention and context, not by law |
| Attention | The eye goes to the brightest, highest-contrast area (see [Continuity and Attention](../EDITING/Continuity-and-Attention.md#6-eye-trace)) |
| Time and place | Low warm sun = late day; cool blue = dusk or night convention; fluorescent green tint = institutional |

## 4. Colour of light

### 4.1 Colour temperature and tint

- **Correlated colour temperature (CCT)**, in kelvin, describes where a light's colour falls on a warm–cool axis (tungsten-like ≈ warm; daylight ≈ neutral-cool).
- **Tint** describes deviation on the green–magenta axis. Many fluorescent and some LED sources have green tints; correcting only CCT leaves skin greenish.

### 4.2 Mixed sources

Real locations mix daylight, tungsten, fluorescent and LED. Strategies:

| Strategy | When | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Unify (gel sources to match, or switch off the odd ones) | Neutral, clean looks; skin consistency | Time, gels, control over practicals |
| Embrace with intent (warm practicals against cool window) | Naturalistic mood; depth through colour contrast | Skin must be lit predominantly by one temperature to look right |
| Choose white balance per scene intent | Usually balanced for the key on faces | Other sources render warm or cool accordingly |

### 4.3 Matching numbers does not guarantee matching colour

Two sources labelled with the same CCT can render skin and fabrics differently because their **spectra** differ (a smooth tungsten spectrum versus a spiky LED or fluorescent spectrum). Metrics such as colour rendering indices and spectral similarity measures describe aspects of this behaviour, but none fully predicts how a specific camera will render a specific skin tone. **Test with the actual camera, sources and people** `[Practice]`. Fixture claims should be checked in manufacturer documentation and on camera, not assumed.

### 4.4 Skin

Skin is the viewer's most sensitive reference. Lighting skin with one dominant, good-spectrum source, keeping other colours as accents, is usually more controllable than mixing equal amounts of different sources on a face. Skin tones vary widely; light and expose for each person, not for an average (see [Correction and Shot Matching](../COLOR/Correction-and-Shot-Matching.md)).

## 5. Lighting connects to everything else

| Connection | Consideration |
|---|---|
| Blocking | Where people stand and move determines where light must be; light and blocking are designed together |
| Exposure | Light levels determine aperture, ISO and noise; high-frame-rate capture needs more light (see [Capture Decisions](../CAMERA/Capture-Decisions.md)) |
| Lenses and depth | Shallow focus needs less background light control; deep focus reveals everything |
| Reflective products | Light the reflections, not just the object |
| Continuity | Window light changes; practicals switched on/off; sun moving — record and plan |
| Time | Daylight windows; schedule critical scenes in stable light |
| Heat and power | Tungsten fixtures are hot; power availability and load limits; patients and vulnerable subjects need comfortable conditions |
| Safety | Stands, cables, heat, water, electrical safety; never compromise for a shot |
| Post latitude | Good lighting gives the colorist options; bad lighting limits them |

**The grade cannot replace acquisition.** It can adjust exposure and colour, shape local brightness and add some apparent direction (see [Texture and Post Relighting](../COLOR/Texture-and-Post-Relighting.md)), but it cannot create real shadows in the right places, restore clipped highlights, remove noise without cost, or change the size of a light source after the fact.

## 6. Low-budget control first

| Problem | Free or cheap control |
|---|---|
| Flat frontal light | Turn the subject so the window is at the side; add black fabric on the far side (negative fill) |
| Bright distracting background | Move the subject; close curtains in the background; flag with cardboard; reframe |
| Harsh sun on face | Move into open shade; use a diffusion frame (even a white bedsheet) between sun and subject |
| Too much contrast on the shadow side | White card or foam board bounce |
| Mixed colour on skin | Switch off overhead fluorescents; use the window as key; keep practicals in the background |
| Unwanted reflections | Change camera angle slightly; move the reflected object; flag the source |

Expensive equipment is valuable when placement and timing are exhausted — large night exteriors, controlled product work, schedule pressure — not as the first move.

## 7. Worked example 1 — Reading the interview room (hypothetical, Project A, location scout at 10:00)

**Observations:**
- One large north-east-facing window on the left wall (as seen from the planned camera position); overcast morning; soft light.
- Overhead fluorescent tubes with a greenish tint. At 25 fps with a 1/50 s shutter in a 50 Hz region, mains flicker is avoided (see [Capture Decisions](../CAMERA/Capture-Decisions.md#6-light-flicker)), but the tint and the flat top-down direction remain.
- A white wall opposite the window bounces light back, filling shadows heavily.
- Back wall: pale yellow, with a shelf and calendar.
- Ceiling fan (noise as well as moving shadows if lit from above).

**Story and attention plan:**
- Key: the window. Asha faces toward the window side, where the interviewer sits beside the lens, and the camera is placed so that the window lights the side of her face turned away from the camera ("short-side" lighting). This gives more facial modelling than lighting the side facing the camera ("broad-side" lighting), which would flatten her face.
- Switch off the overhead fluorescents: removes the green tint and the top-down flattening.
- Hang black fabric on a stand to block the white wall's bounce partially: restores modelling (negative fill).
- Background: keep the shelf a stop or more below her face; if too bright, flag window light from it with a card.
- Schedule: critical questions before early afternoon, when the light may drop or the sun may swing onto the window.

**Expected risk:** clouds passing will vary the light (the "light dip" later found in A001_C014; see [Footage Assessment](../OPERATIONS/Footage-Assessment.md#7-worked-example-1--multicamera-interview-hypothetical-project-a)). Mitigation: monitor exposure; pause for big changes; a B-cam to cut away.

## 8. Worked example 2 — Reading a studio for a reflective product (hypothetical, Project B)

**Observations:** a white-walled studio cove; the product is brushed steel with a glossy lid ring; the lid ring's glow is dim compared with studio light levels.

**Plan:**
- The bottle reflects its environment: white walls everywhere would make it look flat grey. Use large black flags to create dark reflections that define its edges, and long soft sources to create gradient highlights along its length.
- The ring's glow must read: overall light levels around the lid must be low enough that the ring is visibly brighter than its surroundings at the chosen exposure. Light the bottle body with controlled side sources; keep the lid area darker.
- Test the camera's rendering of the ring's colour; LEDs in products can be very saturated and clip in one channel.

**Verification:** test shots at the planned exposure; check the ring is not clipped and reads as glowing on a phone screen.

## 9. Counterexample and repair — implausible source direction

**Hypothetical counterexample.** In a home interview for another documentary, the visible window is on the right of frame, but the crew places a large soft key on the left (where the room is dark) because it "looked nicer". The face is lit from the left, while the window behind on the right is bright. Viewers sense something is off; the image looks "staged", contradicting the observational contract.

**Diagnosis:** the light's direction contradicts the visible motivated source.

**Repair:**
- Move the camera so the window side becomes the key side for the face, or reposition the subject.
- If the left key is needed for exposure, make it a soft fill at a lower level and add a motivated key from the window side (or enhance the window with a fixture outside it or a large bounce near it).
- In post (if no reshoot): reduce the left-side brightness with a soft window, slightly brighten the window side of the face — limited, because shadows fall the wrong way (see [Texture and Post Relighting](../COLOR/Texture-and-Post-Relighting.md)).

**Verification:** compare with the room's actual light; ask a fresh viewer whether the scene looks natural.

## 10. Micro-decisions

- Look at the subject's eyes: a visible catchlight from the motivated source adds life and confirms direction.
- Check the shadow of the nose: it reveals key height and direction instantly.
- Watch for hot spots on foreheads and noses — use powder (with consent) or soften the source.
- Keep the brightest area of the frame on or near the subject unless intentionally otherwise.
- Photograph the lighting setup and note positions for continuity and pickups.
- Measure or note the colour of each source (by camera test or meter) for the colorist.

## 11. Failure signatures and corrections

| Symptom | Likely cause | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Flat, lifeless faces | Frontal light; too much fill; overhead flat light | Side key; negative fill; switch off overheads |
| Distracting bright background | Background closer to the source than the subject | Flag; move subject; reduce background light |
| Inconsistent skin colour between shots | Mixed sources; changing daylight | Unify key colour; monitor daylight; white-balance reference |
| Light from the "wrong" side | Unmotivated key | Align key with visible sources |
| Green/magenta faces | Fluorescent or LED tint | Switch off; correct with gels; white balance carefully |
| Product looks grey or plastic | Uncontrolled reflections | Shape reflections with flags and large sources |

## 12. Verification

- **At intended camera settings:** look through the actual camera, profile and monitoring LUT, at the shooting exposure.
- **Through subject movement:** light that works when the subject is still may fail when they lean or walk.
- **Over the expected shooting period:** check again after an hour, especially with daylight.
- **Against the attention plan:** does the eye go where it should?
