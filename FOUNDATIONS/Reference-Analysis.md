# Reference Analysis: From Surface to Function

**Purpose:** Teach how to analyse a reference film, advertisement or clip so that its *function* can be reused while its *surface* is not copied — and how the same reference produces different decisions in different briefs.

**Applies to:** Pitching, briefing, temp music and look discussions, client moodboards, and any moment someone says "make it like this".

**Prerequisites:** [Creative Brief](Creative-Brief.md).

**Related owners:** Temp-music attachment is treated in [Music Selection](../AUDIO/Music-Selection.md); licensing in [Rights, Consent and Disclosure](../OPERATIONS/Rights-Consent-and-Disclosure.md).

## 1. Why references mislead

A reference is the fastest way for two people to share a feeling, and the fastest way for a team to copy the wrong thing. When a client points at a clip and says "like this", they are usually responding to an *effect on themselves* — they felt excited, reassured, intrigued. The visible features of the clip (its grade, its cuts, its font) are only partly responsible for that effect, and some of them are irrelevant or harmful in a new context.

Reference analysis separates two layers:

- **Surface:** what you can screenshot or name — colour palette, typeface, shot sizes, transition style, track genre, aspect ratio.
- **Function:** what the surface does to the viewer — where attention goes, in what order information arrives, how much is withheld, how performance is framed, how density rises and falls, how sound and picture share work, how the payoff is earned.

Surface is easy to copy and often unoriginal or unlicensed when copied. Function is transferable and original when re-executed in the new material.

## 2. The ten functional layers

Analyse a reference by watching it several times, each time attending to a single layer. Write what you observe, then what it does.

| Layer | Observe | Ask what it does |
|---|---|---|
| Information order | What is shown or said first, second, last? | What question does the opening raise, and when is it answered? |
| Attention | Where is the eye drawn in each shot? What moves, what is brightest, what is a face? | Does the piece guide attention smoothly or deliberately surprise? |
| Performance | How do people speak and move? Are they presenting or living? | What relationship to the viewer does this create — intimacy, authority, play? |
| Visual hierarchy | What dominates the frame: product, face, text, environment? | What does the piece treat as most important? |
| Contrast | Light/dark, still/moving, loud/quiet, dense/sparse | Where are the accents, and what is kept calm so they register? |
| Camera perspective | Height, distance, lens feel, whose point of view | Does the camera observe, participate, admire, interrogate? |
| Motion grammar | How do camera and graphics move — ease, speed, settle, linear? | Does motion feel physical, mechanical, playful, luxurious? |
| Temporal density | How much new information per second, and how it varies | Where does the piece let the viewer breathe? |
| Sound | Voice, music, effects, silence; who carries meaning | Does sound lead picture or follow it? |
| Payoff | What is resolved at the end, and how was it set up? | Is the ending earned by earlier setup or by a musical cadence alone? |

A practical method `[Practice]`: watch once normally, once muted, once with picture covered (audio only), and once scrubbing at the cut points. Each pass reveals a different layer.

## 3. The translation ladder

Once the function is identified, choose how close to stay to the reference.

| Rung | What it means | When acceptable | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Copy | Reproduce the shots, grade, typography and cut pattern | Almost never outside a deliberate, licensed or clearly marked homage or parody | Unoriginal; possible infringement of protected expression; brand confusion |
| Imitate | Same surface with new content | Internal animatics or temp only | Looks derivative; the function may not survive new content |
| Translate | Same function, executed with the new material's own means | Default professional approach | Requires actual analysis; client may initially say "it doesn't look like the reference" |
| Transform | Use the reference as a starting point for a different function | When the reference's function is wrong for the brief but something in it is useful | May lose the client's original feeling if not explained |

The default is **translation**: keep what the reference does, find a way for this material to do it.

## 4. Same reference, different decisions

**Hypothetical reference:** a 40-second running-shoe advertisement the Arc team calls "energetic". On analysis:

- Information order: product appears only after 12 seconds; first, a runner's breath and feet.
- Attention: tight close-ups of body parts, fast lateral movement, very little text.
- Performance: no dialogue; heavy breathing; performance is physical.
- Temporal density: shots of 6–10 frames during the run, then a 3-second hold on the shoe.
- Sound: percussive track with the breathing mixed loud; the music cuts out for the shoe hold.
- Payoff: the hold on the shoe in silence after constant motion.

The *function* the client responds to is **contrast between sustained physical effort and a sudden still, silent product moment**. The surface — fast cutting, sports imagery, percussive music — is only one way to create that contrast.

### Translation for Project B "Arc" 30-second spot (hypothetical)

| Function | Arc execution | Why not copy the surface |
|---|---|---|
| Effort then stillness | A busy morning (keys, laptop, crowded train) cut in a moderate rhythm, then a still moment where the hand lifts Arc and the ring advances | Arc is a calm companion, not a performance product; fast sports cutting would contradict the brand stance in the brief |
| Silence for the product moment | Ambient sound drops to a near-silent room tone; one soft tone accompanies the ring | The silence works; the percussive track would make the bottle feel like gym equipment |
| Body-first information order | Hand and habit first; product revealed through use | Matches "recognition, then small surprise" in the brief |
| Minimal text | One line of text for the claim, required because many viewers are muted | The shoe ad relied on sound-on viewing; Arc cannot |

### Translation for Project A "Second Shift" (hypothetical)

The documentary director is shown the same reference by the nonprofit ("we want energy like this"). The function — sustained effort followed by stillness — actually suits the portrait, but the surface would destroy it.

| Function | Second Shift execution | Why not copy the surface |
|---|---|---|
| Effort then stillness | Long handheld follow during a busy hour, then a locked-off shot of Asha sitting alone at the nurses' station at 04:00 | Fast cutting of a real ward would feel exploitative and would compress real time, which matters to the story |
| Silence for the key moment | The fan and distant generator remain; no music | Removing all ambience would feel artificial; observational contract |
| Body-first | Her hands writing notes, adjusting a drip, before her face | Respect: we meet her through her work |

The same reference produces opposite cutting rates. That is the lesson: **"energetic" is a function to be located in the material, not a tempo to apply.**

## 5. Counterexample and repair: the stylish reference that cost credibility

**Hypothetical counterexample (Project C "Clear Water").** Early in development, the team adopts a polished smartphone-launch film as the visual reference: macro shots with glossy reflections, a dark background, rim light, slow-motion water droplets and sleek kinetic type. The animatic looks impressive. In a fresh-viewer test with five members of the actual audience, three describe it as "an advertisement for an expensive filter" and two ask whether the filter is sold by the channel. Nobody can repeat the third maintenance step.

**Diagnosis.**

- The reference's function was *desire for a premium object*. The brief's function is *trust and competence*: "I understand this and I can do it."
- Glossy surfaces and dark backgrounds signalled a sales context; the audience's prior experience with water-purifier marketing made them suspicious.
- Slow motion and kinetic type consumed time and attention that the maintenance steps needed.

**Repair.**

1. Restate the function: "a trustworthy neighbour showing you how something works on an ordinary kitchen counter."
2. New reference set chosen by function: a respected cooking-demonstration format (clear hands, top-down and side angles, steady pace), and a public-service instructional film with plain labels.
3. Execution: real kitchen counter, soft window-motivated light, hands of an ordinary user, steps numbered with stable labels, no slow motion except where it shows something invisible at normal speed (water passing through the candle is shown in animation instead).
4. Keep one element from the original reference where it served the brief: the clean macro of the candle surface, because it lets viewers see what "dirty" looks like before cleaning.

**Verification.** Repeat the fresh-viewer test. Success: viewers describe the video as instructional, and at least four of five can list the maintenance steps in order. Disconfirming result: if viewers now find it boring and stop watching before the steps, the pacing — not the visual reference — needs work (see [Rhythm and Pacing](../EDITING/Rhythm-and-Pacing.md)).

## 6. Working with references in a team

### 6.1 Labelled moodboards

A moodboard that shows images without labels invites surface copying. Label each reference with the *one function* it is there for and *what not to take* from it.

**Filled example (Project D "The Spare Key", hypothetical):**

| Reference (described, not reproduced) | Take | Do not take |
|---|---|---|
| A domestic drama scene lit only by table lamps | Pools of warm light separating two people in one room; darkness between them as emotional distance | Its very low exposure, which would make our phone-viewing festival audience lose faces |
| A two-hander where the camera does not move until the reveal | Stillness that makes the first camera move significant | Its 9-minute single take; our performers and schedule cannot support it |
| A family film whose silence after a revelation runs several seconds | Permission to hold silence after the key reveal | Its absence of room tone — we keep the refrigerator hum low but present |

### 6.2 Negative references

Ask for one reference the client *dislikes* and analyse it the same way. Negative references often define the boundary more precisely than positive ones: "not this glossy" or "not this preachy" eliminates whole regions of choices.

### 6.3 Temp love

Teams who live with a reference — especially temp music or a temp grade — for weeks begin to hear and see it as the correct answer. The cure `[Practice]`:

- Describe the reference in functional terms early, in writing.
- Rotate temp choices so no single one becomes attached.
- When the final element arrives, judge it against the functional description, not against the temp.

See [Music Selection](../AUDIO/Music-Selection.md#4-temp-track-attachment) for the music-specific version.

## 7. Originality and respect for others' work

- Shots, camera moves, colour palettes and cutting rhythms are generally common vocabulary; a distinctive combination that clearly reproduces a specific work's expression, branded animations, characters, logos, lyrics and music recordings are not. When in doubt, translate further or consult the rights owner — see [Rights, Consent and Disclosure](../OPERATIONS/Rights-Consent-and-Disclosure.md). This is practical guidance, not legal advice.
- Do not imitate a living artist's signature style as a selling point ("in the style of X") for commercial work without considering reputational and legal implications.
- A reference used for a client presentation should be shown as a reference, credited where possible, and not presented as the team's work.

## 8. Micro-decisions

- Time-stamp reference observations ("at 0:12 the product first appears") so discussions point at evidence.
- When a client loves a reference, ask *which second* they love. The answer is often a single moment, not the whole style.
- Watch references on the device the audience will use; a reference that works on a cinema screen may rely on detail invisible on a phone.
- Note what the reference *did not* do (no text, no voice, no logo until the end); absences are often its strongest choices.

## 9. Failure signatures and corrections

| Symptom | Likely cause | Correction | Verification |
|---|---|---|---|
| "It looks like a cheaper version of the reference" | Surface imitation without the reference's resources | Translate the function into what this material does well | Fresh viewers describe it without mentioning the reference |
| Client says "it doesn't feel like the reference" despite similar look | The function was missed (information order, contrast, payoff) | Re-analyse layer by layer; adjust structure, not grade | Client identifies the moment that now "feels right" |
| Credibility drops | The reference's function belongs to a different genre contract | Choose references from the correct genre contract | Trust question in a small viewer test |
| Everything in the edit matches temp and nothing else seems right | Temp love | Functional description; rotate temp | Final element is approved against the description |

## 10. Verification

A reference analysis is useful if it can be summarised in one sentence of function per reference, if it lists what not to take, and if the team can explain how this material will achieve the function differently. Test the result with a fresh viewer who has never seen the reference: if they experience the intended function, the translation worked.
