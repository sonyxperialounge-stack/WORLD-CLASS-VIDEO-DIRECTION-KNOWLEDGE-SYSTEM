# Creative Brief, Intent and Audience

**Purpose:** Teach how an expert turns a vague request ("premium, cinematic, engaging") into explicit communication goals, audience conditions, an emotional trajectory and evidence of success — the document every later decision is tested against.

**Applies to:** Any project before story, shooting or editing decisions are made; and again whenever the objective changes.

**Prerequisites:** [Decision Method](Decision-Method.md).

**Related owners:** Resource trade-offs, priority maps and the brief-change process are in [Constraints and Priorities](Constraints-and-Priorities.md). Reference analysis is in [Reference Analysis](Reference-Analysis.md). Rights and consent formalities are owned by [Rights, Consent and Disclosure](../OPERATIONS/Rights-Consent-and-Disclosure.md).

## 1. What a brief is for

A brief is not a list of adjectives and deliverables. It is the **contract that makes later disagreements decidable.** When the editor and the client argue about whether a cut is "too slow", the brief should already say what the viewer must understand by that point, what they should feel, and what the piece is allowed to sacrifice. Without that, the argument becomes a contest of taste and seniority.

A useful brief answers four questions in plain language:

1. **Who is watching, and in what condition?** Knowledge, language, device, attention, sound on or off, why they are there.
2. **What must change in them?** Understanding, belief, feeling or action — stated so that it can be checked.
3. **What must remain true?** Facts, testimony, product claims, a subject's dignity, accessibility.
4. **What are we allowed to spend?** Time, money, crew, access, rights, review rounds.

Everything else — style, music, pace, look — is an answer to those questions, not part of the question.

## 2. Translating vague language into observable intent

Clients and directors describe outcomes in evaluative adjectives because they experience films that way. The expert's job is not to dismiss the adjectives but to ask what *observable property* would make a viewer use that word, and whether that property suits this piece.

| Word in the request | Possible observable meanings | Diagnostic question to ask | Risk if taken literally |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Premium" | Restraint in motion and text; controlled highlights and reflections; generous negative space; precise sound; few, deliberate claims | "Which existing piece of yours, or a competitor's, feels premium to you, and what would you remove from ours to get there?" | Expensive-looking effects that make a modest product look like it is overcompensating |
| "Cinematic" | Shallow depth of field; motivated camera movement; wider aspect framing; scored emotional arc; considered lighting contrast | "Is the goal a story people feel, or a look people recognise?" | Letterboxing and teal-orange grading pasted onto material that has no dramatic structure |
| "Engaging" | Clear early promise; changing information; human faces and voices; a question the viewer wants answered | "Engaging to whom, and what should they do afterwards?" | Hyperactive cutting that raises attention but lowers comprehension |
| "Authentic" | Imperfect but truthful performance; real locations; visible process; little manipulation | "Which imperfections are acceptable — handheld, ambient noise, hesitation?" | Staged "authenticity" that savvy viewers detect |
| "Energetic" | Rhythmic music; short shots; movement in frame; vocal pace | "Is the energy in the subject, or must we add it?" | Energy imposed on calm material reads as false |
| "Simple" | One message; few elements per frame; no jargon | "What are we willing to leave out?" | Simplicity that removes the proof a sceptical viewer needs |

The output of this translation is a **one-sentence intent** that a third person could test, for example:

> *Hypothetical, Project B "Arc":* "After the 30-second spot, a muted phone viewer who already owns a bottle should be able to say what is different about Arc (it shows hydration progress at a glance) and should perceive the product as precisely made, not gadget-like."

Notice what this sentence does: it names the viewer condition (muted phone), the knowledge change (what is different), the feeling (precisely made) and a negative constraint (not gadget-like). Each part will drive concrete decisions: on-screen text must carry the claim without sound; the reveal must show the lid ring working; motion must be restrained.

## 3. Five layers that must be separated

Most brief conflicts come from treating different kinds of objectives as the same thing. Separate them explicitly.

| Layer | Question | Example (Project A "Second Shift") | Who owns it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Business / organisational objective | Why is anyone paying for this? | The nonprofit wants support for night-staff funding | Commissioner |
| Story objective | What must the piece communicate? | Night nursing in district hospitals depends on a few overstretched people whose work is mostly invisible | Director / editor |
| Viewer experience | What should it feel like to watch? | Quiet, close, unhurried; respect rather than pity | Director |
| Accessibility requirement | Who must be able to access it, and how? | Hindi closed captions; English subtitles; readable on phones; nothing that relies on colour alone | Producer, with accessibility review |
| Delivery constraint | Where, at what spec, by when? | Online series page, social excerpt, projector screening; two-week post | Producer / finishing |

### Resolving conflicts between layers

**Hypothetical conflict:** The nonprofit's communications lead asks for Asha to "say clearly that the hospital needs donations" and for a closing line of music "that makes people want to help." The documentary director's viewer contract is observational truthfulness.

| Option | What it improves | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| A. Ask Asha to say a donation line on camera | Clear call to action | Puts words in a subject's mouth; she becomes a spokesperson, undermining the credibility that makes the piece persuasive; may expose her professionally |
| B. Keep the portrait observational; carry the call to action in an end card written in the organisation's own voice | Separates testimony from advocacy; Asha's account stays hers | The emotional peak and the ask are no longer the same moment |
| C. Use a rising, emotive cue under the final scene | Increases immediate emotional response | Viewers sceptical of charity films discount manipulative music; it tells them what to feel about a person's life |

**Choice:** B, with music restraint (see [Music Selection](../AUDIO/Music-Selection.md)). The organisational objective is better served by credibility than by intensity: an audience that trusts Asha is more likely to trust the ask. **Exception:** if the piece were explicitly an appeal advertisement and Asha had agreed in advance to act as an advocate, a direct statement in her own words — not scripted for her — could be appropriate, disclosed as advocacy. **Validation:** show both endings to a small group; ask what they believe about Asha's motives. If the end-card version leaves viewers unsure what action to take, strengthen the card, not the testimony.

The general rule `[Heuristic]`: when a commercial objective and a truthfulness objective collide, move the commercial element into a channel the viewer recognises as the organisation speaking (end card, voice-over from the organisation, separate call-to-action), rather than bending testimony, performance or evidence.

## 4. The brief worksheet

The worksheet below is a thinking tool, not a gate. A practitioner should be able to start learning and working with half the fields marked "assumed". What matters is that assumptions are visible.

| Field | Why it matters | What a weak answer looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Audience knowledge | Determines orientation, jargon, how much proof is needed | "Everyone" |
| Language(s) | Drives captions, VO, typography, localization budget | "English, maybe others later" (later is expensive) |
| Viewing device and context | Small screen vs projection; sound on/off; lean-in vs scroll | Not stated |
| Desired action or change | The test of success | "Awareness" |
| Important truths | What must not be distorted | Not stated, so nobody can defend them |
| Proof points | Evidence the audience needs to believe the claim | Adjectives instead of evidence |
| Emotional stance | The relationship the piece takes to its subject and viewer | "Inspiring" (inspiring how?) |
| Duration(s) | Shapes structure, not just trim | "As short as possible" |
| Release destinations | Aspect ratios, captions, loudness, interface overlays | "Social" |
| Resource envelope | Crew, days, budget, access, archive | Unknown until late |
| Approvals | Who signs off what, and in what order | "The team" |
| Rights | Talent, music, locations, archive, generated media | "We have a music subscription" |
| Revision rounds | Scope protection | Unlimited |
| Success indicators | How anyone will know it worked | Vanity metrics only |

### Filled example — Project B "Arc" 30-second spot (hypothetical)

| Field | Entry | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Audience knowledge | Owns a reusable bottle; has seen smart bottles before and suspects them of being gimmicks | Confirmed with client research summary |
| Language(s) | English; captions in English | Confirmed |
| Viewing device and context | Phone, in-feed, often muted for the first seconds; secondary: pre-roll on a video platform with sound | Assumed from media plan draft |
| Desired action | Visit the product page; remember the name and the lid-ring idea | Confirmed |
| Important truths | The lid ring shows progress toward a personal target set in the app; it does not measure water quality. Battery life claim of "up to 3 weeks" is under legal review | Confirmed; claim pending |
| Proof points | Close shot of the ring advancing as someone drinks; app screen showing the same progress; brushed-steel detail | Confirmed |
| Emotional stance | Calm confidence; the product is a quiet companion, not a fitness coach shouting at you | Agreed with brand lead |
| Duration(s) | 30 s master; 15 s vertical; 6 s bumper | Confirmed |
| Release destinations | Social feeds (vertical and square), video-platform pre-roll (16:9), product page (16:9) | Confirmed; exact specs to verify per [Platform and Format Strategy](../DELIVERY/Platform-and-Format-Strategy.md) |
| Resource envelope | 2 shoot days; one studio; product handler; motion designer for 6 days; licensed library music | Confirmed |
| Approvals | Brand lead (creative), legal (claims, music), founder (brand film only) | Confirmed |
| Rights | Library music licence must cover paid social and pre-roll worldwide for 12 months — to verify | Open |
| Revision rounds | Two rounds at offline, one at online, one at final | Confirmed |
| Success indicators | Primary: product-page visits from the campaign and recall of the lid-ring feature in a small post-view survey. Secondary: completion rate, interpreted cautiously | Agreed |

**What would change the approach (hinge variables):** if legal rejects the battery claim, the spot loses its second proof point and the middle section must be restructured around the app; if the media plan shifts toward sound-on pre-roll, voice can carry more and on-screen text can be reduced.

### Filled example — Project A "Second Shift" (hypothetical, abbreviated)

| Field | Entry |
|---|---|
| Audience knowledge | General public; most have never been inside a district hospital at night |
| Language(s) | Hindi interview; Hindi closed captions; English subtitles |
| Viewing context | Online series page (laptop and phone), social excerpt, community screenings with a projector and small PA |
| Desired change | Viewers understand that night care depends on a few people, and see Asha as a skilled professional rather than a victim |
| Important truths | Patients are not identifiable without consent; events are shown in the order they happened unless clearly signposted; no reconstruction |
| Emotional stance | Respectful closeness; no pity framing |
| Success indicators | Viewers can describe one specific thing Asha does that they did not know about; the organisation's partners use the film in funding conversations |

## 5. Inferring cautiously from an incomplete brief

Real briefs are incomplete. The expert does not refuse to work until every field is filled; nor do they silently fill gaps with personal taste. They keep an **assumption register** and identify the few variables that would reverse a recommendation.

### The assumption register

| Assumption | Basis | Consequence if wrong | How to confirm cheaply |
|---|---|---|---|
| Most Arc viewers start muted | Typical feed behaviour; media plan draft | If sound-on dominates, text-heavy design wastes attention | Ask the media buyer for the placement split |
| Asha's interview will be in Hindi | Her stated preference | If she code-switches heavily, captions and subtitles must handle mixed language | Note it during the pre-interview |
| The Clear Water audience includes low-literacy viewers | Partner organisation's description | If wrong, the slow, spoken-first design is over-cautious but harmless | Partner confirmation |

### Hinge variables

Most unknowns do not change much. A few change everything. Ask about these first:

- **Sound-on or sound-off first exposure** — reverses the balance between voice and text.
- **Whether testimony may be paraphrased or must remain verbatim** — changes story structure.
- **Whether the piece will be cut into vertical versions** — changes shooting, framing and graphics layout.
- **Whether claims are legally substantiated** — determines which proof points exist.
- **Whether the subject is vulnerable or a minor** — changes consent, interview approach and privacy.

A practitioner who can ask only three questions should ask the three hinge variables most likely to be wrong.

## 6. Preference versus necessity; target versus vanity metric

| Distinction | Test | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Client preference | Would a reasonable viewer be harmed or misled if it were ignored? If not, it is a preference — to be weighed, not obeyed or dismissed | "I like blue." Weigh it against the brand palette and story |
| Technical necessity | Does a specification, law, platform or accessibility requirement compel it? | Captions for the required languages; loudness compliance for broadcast |
| Ethical necessity | Would ignoring it misrepresent a person, fact or evidence? | Not cutting Asha's answer to imply a claim she did not make |
| Useful target | Is it connected to the stated change in the viewer? Can it be measured without gaming? | Recall of the lid-ring feature in a post-view survey |
| Vanity metric | Can it rise while the actual objective falls? | Views from autoplay; completion rate on a 6-second clip |

A practitioner should never promise that a cut style, hook or duration *causes* engagement or sales. Such outcomes depend on distribution, audience, timing, offer and many factors outside the edit. What the edit can control is clarity, credibility, emotional fit and accessibility — and those can be tested with small samples, though small samples do not prove audience-wide effects.

## 7. Emotional trajectory

A brief should describe not just a mood but a **change over time**. "Warm" is a state; "begins curious, becomes concerned, ends reassured with a clear next step" is a trajectory that tells the editor, composer and colorist what each section needs.

| Project | Opening state | Middle | Close | What this means for craft |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A "Second Shift" | Quiet curiosity about an unfamiliar place | Accumulating fatigue and responsibility | Respect; an unresolved but honest question about who cares for carers | Music withheld until late or absent; grade does not beautify fatigue; ending allows silence |
| B "Arc" 30 s | Recognition ("I know this habit") | Small surprise (the ring responds) | Calm confidence | Reveal must be clean and legible; motion settles rather than bounces |
| C "Clear Water" | Concern about water safety | Understanding of the mechanism | Competence; "I can do this" | Pace allows comprehension; demonstration evidence beats animation for trust |
| D "The Spare Key" | Familiar sibling friction | Escalation | A reversal that reframes the argument | Performance holds; silence around the key reveal |

## 8. Counterexample and repair: the brief that defined success as a style

**Hypothetical counterexample.** A regional cooperative bank commissions a 60-second film. The brief reads: "Make it like the viral fast-cut videos everyone is watching. Lots of energy. Gen Z vibe. Success = 1 million views." No audience knowledge, no action, no truths.

The team delivers rapid jump cuts, whip transitions, bold kinetic captions and a trending-style track. Internal reviewers call it "fun". In a five-person fresh-viewer test with the bank's actual target members (small shop owners aged 30–55), four cannot say what the bank offers, and two think it is a payment app.

**Diagnosis.** The brief specified a surface style and a vanity metric. Nothing in it named a viewer change, so nothing in the edit was protecting comprehension. The style signalled youthfulness to an audience that was not young and obscured the one thing the bank needed people to know.

**Repair.**

1. Re-brief with three questions: *Who must act? What must they understand? What makes them distrust banks?* Answers: shop owners; that the bank offers small working-capital loans with a decision in days; distrust of hidden fees.
2. Restate the intent: "A shop owner should be able to repeat the offer and believe there are no hidden fees."
3. Keep energy where the material has it (a real shop owner's quick, funny delivery) and remove imposed energy (whip transitions between unrelated shots).
4. Replace kinetic captions with stable, readable captions plus one clear on-screen statement of the offer.
5. Success indicator: offer recall and stated trust in a small post-view test, plus enquiries — not views.

**Verification.** Repeat the fresh-viewer test with the same audience profile. Success: at least four of five can state the offer unprompted. If they can state it but still distrust it, the problem is proof, not style — add the fee statement in the bank's own voice.

## 9. Micro-decisions in briefing

- Write the one-sentence intent at the top of every version of the brief, the edit decision list cover sheet and the review log. It keeps reviews anchored.
- Record *who* stated each requirement. "The founder said" and "legal requires" carry different weight.
- Use the client's own words for the emotional stance, then attach your observable translation beside them; this avoids the client feeling their language was replaced.
- Ask for a **negative reference** ("what should this definitely not feel like?"). It is often more precise than a positive one.
- Separate "must have by the deadline" from "nice to have if time allows" before the shoot, not after the first cut.
- Date the brief and version it. When it changes, follow the [brief-change process](Constraints-and-Priorities.md#5-the-brief-change-process).

## 10. Failure signatures and corrections

| Symptom | Likely cause | Correction | Verification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reviews argue about taste with no resolution | No stated viewer change to test against | Write the one-sentence intent and get it approved | Next review notes reference the intent |
| Late request for a vertical version breaks the edit | Destinations not captured at brief | Add destinations to the worksheet; plan shooting and framing for them | Version matrix exists before the shoot |
| The piece is clear but nobody acts | No desired action or call-to-action strategy | Define the action and its channel (end card, link, spoken ask) | Fresh viewers can state what to do next |
| Credibility complaints from the audience | Commercial goal bent testimony or evidence | Separate advocacy channel from evidence; restore truthful material | Viewers' trust ratings in a small test |
| Constant scope creep | No revision-round or approval structure | Define rounds and approvers; use the change process | Change requests are logged with impact |

## 11. Verification of a brief

A brief is ready when:

- A person outside the project can read it and state the audience, the change, the truths and the constraints.
- The one-sentence intent can be tested with a fresh viewer.
- Every hinge variable is either confirmed or listed with a cheap confirmation step.
- Rights and approvals have named owners.
- The destinations and language versions are known well enough to plan acquisition.
