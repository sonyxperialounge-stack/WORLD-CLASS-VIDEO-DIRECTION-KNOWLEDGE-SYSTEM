# Decision Method, Evidence Labels and Recurring Projects

**Purpose:** Define the shared method every chapter in this library uses to teach a decision, the labels that separate requirements from heuristics and taste, the units and conventions used in numerical examples, and the four recurring hypothetical projects that carry decisions from chapter to chapter.

**Applies to:** Every reader. Read this before any craft chapter; it is short by design and the rest of the library depends on it.

**Owns:** The decision record format, the confidence labels, the frame/time conversion conventions and the project roster. Other chapters link here rather than redefining them.

---

## 1. Why a method instead of a list of rules

Experienced editors, colorists and mixers rarely work from fixed rules. They work from a small number of questions they ask repeatedly: *What is this moment for? What does the viewer already know? What is the evidence in front of me? What are the real alternatives, and what does each one cost?* The answers differ from project to project, which is why rule lists ("always cut on action", "always use three-point lighting", "always mix to −14 LUFS") fail as soon as the context changes.

This library therefore teaches **observable decision systems**: what to look at or listen for, which questions change the answer, what the competing options are, how to choose, how to check the choice, and when the opposite choice is better. It does not claim to reproduce any named professional's private thinking. It describes criteria that any practitioner can inspect, argue about and test.

## 2. The decision pattern

Every consequential topic in this library is taught with the substance of the following fifteen elements. Small topics use only the elements that matter; large topics use all of them at least once.

| # | Element | Question it answers | Typical mistake it prevents |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Purpose | What narrative, perceptual, emotional or operational problem does this decision solve? | Applying a technique because it is available |
| 2 | Observable inputs | What exactly should be inspected in picture, sound, performance, brief or spec? | Deciding from memory or habit rather than the material |
| 3 | Diagnostic questions | Which answers would change the choice? | Treating every case as identical |
| 4 | Options | What are the real alternatives, including doing nothing? | False binary choices; over-intervention |
| 5 | Selection criteria | How do the options differ *in this context*? | Choosing the option one is most practised at |
| 6 | Trade-offs | What improves, what costs more, what becomes riskier? | Hidden downstream costs |
| 7 | Recommendation | What would be chosen for a stated example, and why? | Endless "it depends" without commitment |
| 8 | Boundary conditions | When would a different choice be better? | Turning a good example into a false universal law |
| 9 | Micro-decisions | Which small details change perception? | Correct concept, clumsy execution |
| 10 | Failure signatures | What does weak execution look or sound like? | Not recognising the problem when it appears |
| 11 | Correction | Which intervention matches which cause? | Treating symptoms with more processing |
| 12 | Verification | What to compare, watch, listen to or measure afterwards | Assuming a change worked |
| 13 | Dependencies | What must be settled earlier, or updated elsewhere? | Fixing one department and breaking another |
| 14 | Worked example and counterexample | How does the idea behave in applied cases? | Abstract slogans |
| 15 | Confidence | Is this a requirement, practice, heuristic or preference? | Presenting taste as law |

### 2.1 Using the pattern in practice

You do not need to write fifteen headings to make a decision. On a real job the pattern compresses into three habits:

1. **Name the job before the tool.** "The lower third must identify the speaker without pulling the eye off her face during the first sentence" is a job. "Animate the lower third" is not.
2. **Hold at least two live options.** If you can only think of one way to solve the problem, you have not yet understood the problem. The simplest option, including no intervention, is always one of the candidates.
3. **Decide how you will know it worked before you do it.** Verification designed after the change tends to confirm whatever was done.

## 3. The decision record

For complex choices this library uses a compact public record. It is a teachable explanation of criteria and outcome, not a transcript of private reasoning.

| Field | What belongs here |
|---|---|
| Intent | What the audience should understand, notice or feel |
| Evidence | Observable facts, or clearly labeled hypothetical facts |
| Constraint | Duration, resources, rights, access, platform or technical limitation |
| Alternatives | Two or more credible options when the choice warrants comparison |
| Choice | Recommended action and concise rationale |
| Trade-off | What improves and what becomes harder or weaker |
| Exception | Evidence that would reverse the recommendation |
| Validation | Specific comparison or acceptance condition |

### Filled example (hypothetical, Project A "Second Shift")

| Field | Entry |
|---|---|
| Intent | Viewers should feel the fatigue of the nurse's shift before she names it, so that her later statement "I stopped noticing I was tired" lands as an admission rather than a complaint. |
| Evidence | *Hypothetical:* a 41-second handheld shot of her walking the ward at 03:10, with an audible ceiling fan and one patient's cough; the interview line occurs at 4:12 in the assembly. |
| Constraint | Six-minute total duration; no second night of filming is available. |
| Alternatives | (1) Cut the walk to 8 seconds as a transition. (2) Hold 20–25 seconds with only ambient sound. (3) Hold 20 seconds under music. |
| Choice | Option 2. The duration and the absence of music let the viewer experience the hour; music would tell them what to feel and pre-empt the admission. |
| Trade-off | Costs roughly 15 seconds that must come from elsewhere (a repeated explanation of shift rotation is the candidate); risks viewer impatience if the shot lacks internal events. |
| Exception | If fresh viewers report boredom *before* the cough at 0:14 of the shot, shorten to the cough and its aftermath; if the walk contains no internal event at all, Option 1 is better. |
| Validation | Two fresh viewers watch the assembly at full speed; afterwards ask "what was the night like for her?" Success means they describe tiredness or loneliness in their own words before being asked about the interview line. |

A blank version of this record and the other reusable forms are collected in [Worksheets and Logs](../TEMPLATES/Worksheets-and-Logs.md).

## 4. Confidence labels

Every important claim in this library carries, explicitly or by clear context, one of five labels. They let a reader know how hard to push back.

| Label | Meaning | How to treat it | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| `[Requirement]` | A verified external specification from a standards body, platform or regulator, recorded in [Sources and Limitations](../SOURCES-AND-LIMITATIONS.md) | Comply, but re-check the current version before claiming compliance | EBU R 128-2023 sets a programme loudness target of −23.0 LUFS for broadcast normalisation |
| `[Practice]` | Established professional practice with broad agreement, but not a formal requirement | Follow unless you have a stated reason not to | Keep original camera media and at least one independent verified copy before formatting cards |
| `[Heuristic]` | A context-dependent craft guideline with a rationale and known failure cases | Use as a starting point, test against the material | A reaction shot usually needs to begin slightly before the listener visibly responds |
| `[Preference]` | Aesthetic choice; reasonable experts disagree | Decide by brief and coherence, not by authority | A slightly warm skin rendering in an intimate documentary |
| `[Illustrative]` | A number or condition assumed so an example can be concrete | Never copy into a delivery spec without checking | "Assume a 1920×1080 frame and a 54 px caption height" |

Two rules keep the labels honest:

- **No invented precision.** Where a number would only decorate an opinion, this library gives the reasoning instead. When numbers help, their units and assumptions are stated.
- **No invented citations.** External specifications are cited only when they were checked; otherwise the text names the *class of authority* that settles the question (standards body, platform specification page, accessibility guideline, manufacturer documentation) so the reader can verify it later.

## 5. Evidence categories when discussing material

Because no real footage is supplied with this library, every observation about pictures or sound is hypothetical unless stated otherwise. The library distinguishes:

| Category | Wording used | What it can support |
|---|---|---|
| Hypothetical observation | "*Hypothetical:* the boom track has a 50 Hz hum at roughly −50 dBFS" | Teaching a decision; never a claim about real media |
| Measured fact (real projects only) | "Measured with a BS.1770-compliant meter: −23.4 LUFS integrated" | Compliance claims, if the tool and settings are recorded |
| Viewer evidence | "Two of three fresh viewers could not say who the speaker was" | Evidence about comprehension in that small sample, not about all audiences |
| Specification | "The delivery document requires …" | Compliance with that document, for that version |
| Judgment | "The cut feels early because the thought has not finished" | A testable professional opinion; it should name the cue it relies on |

When this library is applied to a real project (see [Project Application](#8-applying-this-library-to-a-real-project)), the same categories apply: a practitioner who has not actually watched a clip at full speed must not describe its motion as smooth.

## 6. Units, frames and time

Many chapters use frame counts. The following conversions are exact or rounded as noted. They are arithmetic, not cutting laws.

| Frame rate | Duration of 1 frame | 3 frames | 6 frames | 12 frames | Common use in this library |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 23.976 fps (24000/1001) | ≈ 41.7 ms | ≈ 125 ms | ≈ 250 ms | ≈ 500 ms | Projects B and D |
| 24 fps | 41.67 ms | 125 ms | 250 ms | 500 ms | Cinema reference |
| 25 fps | 40 ms | 120 ms | 240 ms | 480 ms | Project A |
| 29.97 fps (30000/1001) | ≈ 33.4 ms | ≈ 100 ms | ≈ 200 ms | ≈ 400 ms | Project C |
| 30 fps | 33.3 ms | 100 ms | 200 ms | 400 ms | Screen recordings |
| 50 fps | 20 ms | 60 ms | 120 ms | 240 ms | High-rate capture for 25 fps projects |
| 59.94 fps | ≈ 16.7 ms | ≈ 50 ms | ≈ 100 ms | ≈ 200 ms | High-rate capture for 29.97/23.976 projects |

Further conventions used throughout:

- **Timecode** is written `HH:MM:SS:FF` for non-drop-frame and `HH:MM:SS;FF` (semicolon) for drop-frame. Drop-frame timecode exists only for 29.97 and 59.94 fps; it skips frame *numbers* (not frames) so that the timecode stays close to clock time. The project's convention is decided and documented at ingest; see [Ingest and Preparation](../OPERATIONS/Ingest-and-Preparation.md).
- **Audio levels:** dBFS is a sample-level scale relative to digital full scale; dBTP is an estimated inter-sample (true) peak; LUFS/LKFS is a loudness measure over time defined by ITU-R BS.1770; LU is a relative loudness difference. They are not interchangeable. See [Mix Strategy](../AUDIO/Mix-Strategy.md) and [Audio Delivery](../DELIVERY/Audio-Delivery.md).
- **Image levels:** code values are given as 10-bit (0–1023) or as percentages of the signal range, and the text states whether a figure is scene-referred (camera/log) or display-referred (after the output transform). See [Color Management](../COLOR/Color-Management.md).
- **Codec vs container:** a codec compresses (H.264, ProRes, DNxHR); a container wraps streams (MOV, MP4, MXF). The library never uses them interchangeably.
- **Times in example timelines** are illustrative and labeled with the frame rate.

## 7. Recurring hypothetical projects

Four fictional projects recur across chapters so that a decision made in one department can be followed into others. Their assumptions are fixed here; chapters may add detail but do not contradict these facts. Each is developed end-to-end in [Worked Projects](../WORKED-PROJECTS/00-Overview.md).

### Project A — "Second Shift" (documentary segment)

| Attribute | Assumption |
|---|---|
| Format | 6-minute documentary segment for a nonprofit health organisation's online documentary series; also projected at community screenings |
| Subject | Asha, a night-shift nurse at a district hospital in northern India; first time on camera; nervous; speaks Hindi |
| Viewer contract | Truthful observational portrait; no reconstruction; music used sparingly |
| Coverage | One interview (about 70 minutes recorded, two cameras: mirrorless A-cam on a 50 mm-equivalent framing, phone B-cam at a 3/4 angle), two hours of handheld observational footage on one night, a few establishing exteriors |
| Capture | 25 fps, 1/50 s shutter, camera log profile; interview window-lit by day; ward lit by overhead fluorescent tubes and a desk lamp at night |
| Sound | Lavalier and boom on the interview; ceiling fan, distant generator hum and corridor traffic present; on-camera mic only during the night follow |
| Deliverables | 16:9 1920×1080 master; Hindi closed captions; English subtitles; a 60-second 9:16 excerpt for social; a projection copy |
| Constraints | One editor who also grades and mixes; two-week post schedule; no reshoot nights; consent obtained from Asha and the hospital, patients must not be identifiable without separate consent |

### Project B — "Arc" (product and brand campaign)

| Attribute | Assumption |
|---|---|
| Product | Arc, a brushed-steel insulated smart bottle whose lid ring glows to show hydration progress, paired with a phone app |
| Deliverables | 90-second founder-led brand film (16:9); 30-second product spot (16:9); 15-second 9:16 vertical; 6-second bumper; all in English with burned-in and sidecar captions |
| Audience | Urban professionals aged roughly 25–45 who already own a reusable bottle; most viewing on phones, often muted at first |
| Capture | 23.976 fps master timeline; 1/48 s base shutter; selected product moves captured at 59.94 and 119.88 fps for retiming; controlled studio for product, founder interview in the company's office |
| Look | Clean, warm-neutral studio look; founder material more naturalistic |
| Constraints | Two-day shoot; licensed music from a production library; phone-screen app UI must be replaced in post with approved final UI; product claims must be substantiated by the client's legal review |

### Project C — "Clear Water" (educational explainer)

| Attribute | Assumption |
|---|---|
| Topic | How a household gravity water filter works and how to maintain it safely |
| Format | 5-minute explainer for a public-health education channel; produced in Hindi and English language versions |
| Audience | Households with mixed literacy; many viewers on low-cost phones with small speakers; some viewers read Hindi more comfortably than English and vice versa |
| Material | Tabletop demonstration shot under controlled light; animated cross-section diagram; one bar chart of hypothetical lab test results; narrator voice-over; on-screen labels |
| Capture | 29.97 fps, non-drop-frame timecode (stated because the screen-recorded app segment and music edit share the same clock) |
| Constraints | Factual claims reviewed by a public-health advisor; captions required in both languages; tight budget; narration can be re-recorded but the demonstration cannot be reshot |

### Project D — "The Spare Key" (narrative scene)

| Attribute | Assumption |
|---|---|
| Story | A three-minute night scene: two adult siblings, Ravi and Nisha, argue about selling their late father's flat; the reveal of a spare key he gave only to one of them shifts the scene |
| Coverage | Master wide, two medium singles, two over-the-shoulder shots, inserts of the key and a photo frame; three to six takes per setup |
| Capture | 23.976 fps; base shutter 1/48 s, changed on set to 1/50 s for scenes lit by LED practicals to avoid mains flicker (see [Capture Decisions](../CAMERA/Capture-Decisions.md#9-worked-example-1--led-practical-flicker-hypothetical-project-d-23976-fps-in-a-50-hz-region)); practical lamps as motivated sources; small crew |
| Sound | Boom-led production sound; a refrigerator hum the crew could not switch off in two takes |
| Deliverables | Festival short master (16:9, 2.39:1 framing inside 1.78:1 delivery), English subtitles for the Hindi-English mixed dialogue |

## 8. Applying this library to a real project

When real material exists, the same method applies with one change: hypothetical observations are replaced by actual ones, and their limits are stated. A practical sequence:

1. Fill the [creative brief](Creative-Brief.md) from the real request, marking assumptions.
2. Run the [footage assessment](../OPERATIONS/Footage-Assessment.md) on the actual media.
3. Use the relevant craft chapters as decision references, writing decision records for the consequential choices.
4. Use the [Master Checklist](../MASTER-CHECKLIST.md) as the gate sequence.
5. Record what was measured, with which tool, and what was only judged by eye or ear.
