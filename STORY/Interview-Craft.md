# Interview Craft and Testimony

**Purpose:** Teach the interview as designed story acquisition — how to derive questions from the communication goal, conduct the conversation so that truthful, usable material emerges, capture it so the edit has options, and protect the subject's meaning from the first session to picture lock.

**Applies to:** Documentary, brand films with real people, explainers with experts, vox-pops, accountability interviews, testimonials and remote interviews.

**Prerequisites:** [Creative Brief](../FOUNDATIONS/Creative-Brief.md), [Decision Method](../FOUNDATIONS/Decision-Method.md).

**Ownership boundaries:** Consent forms, releases, minors and disclosure formalities are owned by [Rights, Consent and Disclosure](../OPERATIONS/Rights-Consent-and-Disclosure.md). Editing of recorded answers is owned by [Dialogue and Voice](../AUDIO/Dialogue-and-Voice.md) (sound) and [Performance Editing](../EDITING/Performance-Editing.md) (selection and assembly). Story structure built from the answers is in [Story Architecture](Story-Architecture.md).

---

## 1. The interview is where the story is captured

In testimony-driven work, the edit can only contain what the interview captured. No amount of cutting will produce a specific memory the subject was never asked to recall, a moment of reflection that the schedule never allowed, or an answer the subject would recognise as theirs if they were pressured into it. The interview is therefore an editorial act performed in advance.

An expert does not arrive with a questionnaire to be completed. They arrive with:

1. **A viewer contract** — what the audience must learn, feel and be able to verify.
2. **A listening plan** — the few answers the piece genuinely needs, the follow-up directions worth pursuing, and the questions that must wait.
3. **A capture plan** — framing, sound and duration decisions that keep editorial options open.
4. **An ethical frame** — what has been agreed with the subject, what is off-limits, and how their meaning will be protected.

### Selecting someone to interview

Who is chosen is itself an editorial act, performed before the first question, and it constrains everything the edit can later do. A subject is worth approaching when they can speak to the piece's claim from direct experience, and when they can do so without being harmed by it.

| Test | Ask | If the answer is weak |
|---|---|---|
| Direct experience | Did they see, do or live the thing — rather than read about it? | Find a witness; use the expert for context, not for testimony |
| Recall, not fluency | Can they take you back to a specific moment, even haltingly? | Prefer the halting witness over the polished spokesperson |
| Access | Can they be reached, and are they willing to be filmed where the thing actually happens? | Reconsider the location, or the subject |
| Exposure | Could being identified cost them a job, a relationship or their safety? | Agree protections first, or choose someone the material cannot harm |
| Representativeness (vox-pop and survey modes) | Does the chosen range match the actual spread, not only the vivid extremes? | Deliberately recruit the middle as well as the edges |

**Fluency is a bias, not a qualification** `[Heuristic]`: producers gravitate to confident speakers, which quietly turns a film into the testimony of the most articulate rather than the most relevant. Where the story belongs to someone who speaks slowly, or in another register or language, that is an editing problem, not a reason to choose someone else.

**Selection is an approach, and the approach is a consent decision.** How a subject is invited, what they are told about the use of the material before a camera is opened, and what happens if they decline or later withdraw are governed by [Rights, Consent and Disclosure](../OPERATIONS/Rights-Consent-and-Disclosure.md). The interviewer's responsibility at this stage is honesty about use; the producer's is documentation.

## 2. Deriving questions from the communication goal

Begin with the brief's one-sentence intent and work backwards.

| Viewer must… | Therefore the interview must capture… | Question style that tends to produce it |
|---|---|---|
| Learn a fact | A clear, self-contained statement in the subject's words | "Walk me through what happens when…" |
| Feel something | A specific memory told as a scene, with sensory detail | "Take me back to the night when… What did you see first?" |
| Verify a claim | Concrete evidence: numbers, examples, the subject's direct experience rather than opinion | "How do you know that? Give me an example you saw yourself." |
| Trust the speaker | Moments of hesitation, qualification and self-correction | Questions that invite nuance: "What's the part of this that's complicated?" |
| Understand stakes | What would be lost or what changed | "What would happen if you weren't there?" |

### The listening plan — filled example (hypothetical, Project A "Second Shift")

| Category | Content |
|---|---|
| Answers the piece needs (no more than five) | 1. What a typical night looks like, as a sequence. 2. One specific patient moment that stayed with her (without identifying the patient). 3. What she does that nobody sees. 4. How the shift affects her outside work. 5. Why she continues. |
| Follow-up directions worth pursuing | Any mention of a colleague; any contradiction between "I'm used to it" and a specific memory that suggests otherwise; humour |
| Questions that must wait | Anything about staffing shortages or management (risk to her job; agreed in advance to handle carefully); family circumstances (only if she raises them) |
| Off-limits by agreement | Names or identifying details of patients; criticism of named colleagues |
| What success sounds like | Specific scenes in her own words, at her own pace, including at least one moment she did not plan to say |

The plan is short on purpose. A list of forty questions turns the interviewer into a reader, and the subject into a respondent.

## 3. Ordering the conversation

Order changes what people say. The same question asked in minute three and in minute forty produces different answers, because the subject's state has changed.

| Stage | Purpose | Examples | Why here |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arrival and setup (not recorded, or recorded as reference only) | Rapport; explain process; reduce anxiety | Small talk; show the camera monitor; explain that mistakes are normal | Nervous subjects calm when the process becomes visible |
| Warm-up (recorded) | Get comfortable speaking; find their natural voice | "Tell me about your morning before the shift." Factual, low-stakes | Early answers are often stiff and rarely used; that is acceptable |
| Chronology | Establish the factual spine | "When did you start nursing? What was your first night like?" | Chronology is easier to recall than interpretation, and it surfaces scenes |
| Specific scenes | Emotional material | "Tell me about a night you still think about." | Needs established trust and recall momentum |
| Interpretation | Meaning and reflection | "Why do you think you stayed?" | Reflection is richer after the subject has relived specific moments |
| Sensitive material (if agreed) | Difficult topics | Pre-agreed areas, introduced with an explicit opt-out | Trust is highest; subject knows they can stop |
| Close | Open door; anything missed | "Is there anything I should have asked?" | Often produces the best answer of the day |

**Chronology before interpretation** `[Practice]`: asking "why" before "what happened" invites rehearsed opinions. Asking "what happened" first lets interpretation arise from memory.

**Sensitive questions last or by prior agreement** `[Practice]`: an early sensitive question can make the rest of the interview guarded. Exceptions: a time-limited accountability interview, where the key question must be asked before the subject can leave; or a subject who wants to address the difficult topic first to get it over with — ask them.

## 4. Question design

### 4.1 Open questions that can surprise

| Weak | Why weak | Stronger | Why stronger |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Was it a hard night?" | Yes/no; suggests the answer | "What was that night like?" | Lets the subject choose the frame |
| "Would you say it was a turning point?" | Leading; supplies the phrase | "What changed after that?" | The subject finds their own words |
| "How do you feel about the hospital and your colleagues?" | Double-barrelled | Two separate questions | One thought per answer is easier to edit |
| "Why is hydration important?" (Arc founder) | Invites a generic talking point | "When did you first notice you weren't drinking enough water?" | Produces a specific, ownable story |
| "Tell me everything about the filter." (Clear Water expert) | Too broad; produces a lecture | "What do people most often get wrong when cleaning it?" | Focuses on what the audience needs |

### 4.2 Asking for scenes, not summaries

Summaries ("It was challenging but rewarding") are rarely usable. Scenes ("It was 3 a.m., the power cut out, and I had a torch in my mouth while…") carry meaning and emotion. Prompt for scenes with: *"Take me there." "What did you see first?" "Who else was in the room?" "What did you say?"*

### 4.3 Full-sentence answers: a real trade-off

Many interviewers ask subjects to "include the question in your answer" so that answers stand alone without the question audio. This is a genuine trade-off, not a rule.

| Option | Gains | Costs | Choose when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ask for full-sentence answers | Answers usable without the question; flexible structure | Answers become stiff and rehearsed; nervous subjects become more nervous; the "including the question" phrase often sounds unnatural | Brand testimonials; experts comfortable on camera; when the interviewer's voice will not appear |
| Let subjects answer naturally; record question audio cleanly | Natural speech | Some answers depend on the question and cannot stand alone | Observational documentary; nervous subjects |
| Natural answers, then gently re-ask for a standalone version only of key lines | Mix of naturalness and usability | Risk of the second version sounding rehearsed | When a few crucial statements must stand alone |

For Project A, the third option is chosen: Asha answers naturally; for two key statements the director later says, "That was beautiful — could you tell me that again from the beginning, as if I didn't know anything about it?" This is *asking again for clarity*, not asking for different content. See [Section 5.3](#53-asking-again-versus-manufacturing) for the line between the two.

## 5. Working with non-professional subjects

### 5.1 Rapport before recording

People speak truthfully when they feel safe and respected. Practical steps:

- Meet before the shoot day if possible, without a camera.
- Explain in plain language how the material will be used: where it will be shown, who will see it, what languages, whether short clips will appear on social media. Formal consent is documented separately by the producer (see [Rights, Consent and Disclosure](../OPERATIONS/Rights-Consent-and-Disclosure.md)); the interviewer's job is to make sure the subject actually understands.
- Agree in advance on sensitive areas and how to stop or skip a question.
- On the day, keep the crew small, introduce everyone, and let the subject see the monitor if they want.

### 5.2 Managing nervousness

| Symptom | What helps | What makes it worse |
|---|---|---|
| Rushed speech, short answers | Slow your own pace; ask about concrete, easy memories; reassure that pauses will be cut | Telling them to "relax"; showing impatience |
| Looking at the camera instead of the interviewer | Sit close to the lens axis at eye height; maintain eye contact; nod | Moving around; looking at notes |
| Stiff, formal language | Respond like a conversation; laugh where appropriate; ask follow-ups about their words | Reading questions off paper |
| Freezing after a mistake | "That's completely normal, we have plenty of time; just start the sentence again whenever you're ready." | Saying "cut" loudly; crew discussing the mistake |

### 5.3 Asking again versus manufacturing

There is a clear ethical line between two practices that can look similar on set:

- **Asking again for clarity:** the subject said something true but tangled, interrupted, or inaudible; you ask them to say it again in their own words. The content is theirs; only the delivery is cleaner.
- **Reshooting until they parrot preferred phrasing:** the subject said something true but not what the producer wanted; you keep asking, suggesting words, until they say the desired sentence. The content becomes yours.

The test `[Practice]`: *Would the subject, watching the final film, recognise the statement as what they meant?* If you had to supply the key words, the answer is probably no.

### 5.4 Interviewer energy, pace and silence

The interviewer is part of the performance. Fast, energetic interviewers get fast, energetic answers — sometimes shallow ones. Slow, attentive interviewers get slower, more reflective answers. Neither is correct in general.

The single most useful technique is **patient silence** `[Heuristic]`: after an answer seems complete, wait two or three seconds longer than feels comfortable, while maintaining attentive eye contact. Subjects often fill the silence with the most honest thing they say all day. The technique fails with very anxious subjects, for whom silence feels like judgement — read the person.

### 5.5 Why feeding words fails

"Would you say it was a turning point?" invites the answer "Yes, it was a turning point." The result is fluent but untruthful in a subtle way: the framing belongs to the interviewer. Audiences cannot always identify why, but such sound-bites tend to sound generic, and the subject may later not recognise them. Worse, in factual work they turn the interviewer's hypothesis into apparent testimony.

## 6. Capture planning as an editorial decision

### 6.1 Location

Choose the location for sound first, then light, then background meaning. A beautiful but reverberant hall produces beautiful but hard-to-understand testimony; dialogue restoration cannot fully recover what was never captured cleanly (see [Recording and Cleanup](../AUDIO/Recording-and-Cleanup.md)).

| Factor | Check | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Noise | Listen for 30 seconds with headphones and the recording chain; fans, fridges, traffic, HVAC | Choose another room; ask permission to switch off appliances; schedule around traffic |
| Reverberation | Clap test; listen for tail | Soft furnishings, blankets off-camera, closer mic |
| Light | Window direction and changes over the shooting period | Position subject relative to window; see [Practical Setups](../LIGHTING/Practical-Setups.md) |
| Background | Does it say something true about the subject? Is it distracting? | Reposition; reduce clutter; avoid branding conflicts |
| Control | Will people walk through? Phones? | Signs, a crew member at the door |

### 6.2 Eye-line and framing

| Eye-line | Effect | Use | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Off-axis to an interviewer beside the lens | Observed conversation; viewer watches the subject speak to someone | Most documentary testimony; conversational modes | Too far off-axis makes the subject feel distant |
| Direct to lens (via a teleprompter-style interview mirror or a remote setup) | Direct address; intimacy or confrontation | Testimony addressed to the audience; some brand films | Many non-professionals find it unnatural; can feel like a presentation |
| Changing during the interview | Inconsistent | Avoid unless intended | Edit cannot intercut answers smoothly |

Decide eye-line deliberately and keep it consistent for answers that will be intercut.

### 6.3 Coverage for the edit

- **Two cameras** (or one camera plus a second angle) allow cutting within an answer without a visible jump. Project A uses a mirrorless A-cam and a phone B-cam at a 3/4 angle.
- **Alternate framing for vertical versions:** if a 9:16 version is planned, either frame the A-cam with enough headroom and side space for a vertical extraction, or shoot a second camera framed for vertical. Indiscriminate center framing weakens both versions — see [Composition and Optics](../CAMERA/Composition-and-Optics.md).
- **Cutaway relief:** plan B-roll that relates to specific answers (Asha's hands writing notes), not random "pretty shots".
- **Handles:** let the camera roll a few seconds before and after each answer. Pre- and post-roll give the editor room for breaths, reactions and J/L cuts.

### 6.4 Sound

- Record room tone (at least 30 seconds of the room with everyone still) at each setup, and again if the room changes.
- Record the interviewer on a separate microphone, even if the voice will not be used. Question audio is needed for transcripts, for context checks, and sometimes for an audible-question style.
- Ask the interviewer to respond with **silent nods** rather than "mm-hmm" during answers. Audible affirmations on the subject's track are difficult to remove cleanly.
- Decide whether audible reactions (laughter, a sigh from the interviewer) will be kept. Kept reactions place the interviewer in the film; cleaned reactions make the subject's testimony stand alone. This is a viewer-contract decision, not a technical one.

### 6.5 Duration and fatigue

People tire. Attention, energy and voice quality drop after a sustained period, and the drop is uneven. Plan breaks; watch for signs (shorter answers, repeated phrases, looking away). A two-hour interview without breaks often yields its best material in the first half and its most honest material just after a break, when the subject relaxes. Protect **schedule slack** after the planned questions: the most useful moment often arrives after the prepared answer ends.

### 6.6 Interviewer on camera or not

| Option | Viewer contract | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Interviewer invisible and inaudible | Subject speaks "to the audience" | Requires standalone answers; less transparency about how answers were elicited |
| Interviewer audible, not visible | Transparent questioning; conversational | Question audio must be recorded cleanly |
| Interviewer visible | The relationship is part of the story | Needs coverage of the interviewer; changes performance |

### 6.7 Removing question audio

When question audio must be removed, plan cut points: subjects often begin answering before the question finishes. Ask the subject (gently, during setup) to leave a small pause after a question. If overlap remains, the editor can cut on a breath in the answer or use a cutaway to cover a slight trim — see [Dialogue and Voice](../AUDIO/Dialogue-and-Voice.md).

## 7. Remote interviews

Remote interviews change both questioning and post options.

| Constraint | Effect | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Eye-line | Subject looks at the interviewer's face on screen, below or beside the camera | Ask them to place the video window directly under the lens; or accept off-lens eye-line consistently |
| Latency | Overlapping speech; interrupted pauses; patient silence becomes ambiguous | Slow your pace; signal with a nod before speaking; avoid rapid follow-ups |
| Compression of the call stream | Blocky picture, artefacts in motion, unstable audio | Record locally on the subject's side (a separate local recording on their device, sent afterwards) — a "double-ender" approach |
| Subject's environment | Poor light, noise, unstable connection | A pre-call to set up light (face a window), choose a quiet room, use wired headphones |
| Interpreter logistics | Delays double; interpreter voice must be separable | Record the subject's original audio separately; schedule extra time; agree on consecutive vs simultaneous interpretation in advance |

Local recording means the call stream is only a monitoring channel; the final picture and sound come from the subject's device. Check how the file will be transferred and whether the subject is comfortable doing it; data-handling and consent apply (see [Rights, Consent and Disclosure](../OPERATIONS/Rights-Consent-and-Disclosure.md)).

## 8. Interview modes and their viewer contracts

| Mode | Viewer contract | Coverage | Sound | Lighting | Performance selection | Edit strategy |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Talking-head testimony | "This person is telling you their experience." | One or two angles; consistent eye-line | Clean lav and boom; room tone | Naturalistic, consistent | Truthfulness over fluency | Structure from answers; B-roll illustrates specifics |
| Conversational two-hander | "You are overhearing a real exchange." | Both speakers covered; a two-shot | Two isolated mics | Matched between speakers | Listening and reactions matter | Keep overlaps that carry meaning; cut on reactions |
| Expert explainer | "This person knows and will explain." | A-cam plus demonstration coverage | Clean, close | Clear, authoritative but not cold | Clarity and accuracy | Interleave with demonstration and graphics |
| Vox-pop | "A range of ordinary voices." | Many short setups; varied backgrounds | Directional mic; noise accepted | Available light | Variety and representativeness — avoid choosing only extremes | Rhythm of short answers; balance of views must not misrepresent the actual spread |
| Accountability exchange | "Someone is being asked to answer for something." | Both parties; wide enough to show the exchange | Full record of both sides | Neutral; not unflattering by design | Full answers, including evasions | Never cut a response to a different question; keep context of each answer |

## 9. Follow-up listening and live logging

During the interview, the director (or a dedicated logger) notes timecode and a few words for every standout moment: a surprising answer, a laugh, a strong scene, a contradiction, an emotional shift. These notes save hours in post and protect against the transcript-only trap — a transcript does not record that the subject paused for four seconds before answering. The live log also seeds the paper edit: its ratings and warnings travel with each line into the assembly plan (see [Paper Edit and Assembly](../OPERATIONS/Paper-Edit-and-Assembly.md)).

**Filled live log excerpt (hypothetical, Project A, 25 fps timecode):**

| TC (A-cam) | Note | Rating |
|---|---|---|
| 10:14:22:10 | Describes torch-in-mouth during power cut — strong scene, slight laugh | ★★★ |
| 10:31:05:00 | Long pause (≈4 s) before "I stopped noticing I was tired" | ★★★ — protect pause |
| 10:42:18:12 | Mentions colleague Sunita covering for her — follow-up worthwhile; check consent if named | ★★ |
| 10:55:40:00 | After "is there anything I should have asked?": talks about her daughter asking why she sleeps in the day | ★★★ — unplanned; potential opening |

## 10. Ethics from the first session

Ethics is not a post-production review step; it starts with the first question.

- **Preserve hesitancy, refusal and nuance.** If a subject says "I think so, mostly, but not always," the edit must not reduce it to "I think so." Qualifications are part of meaning.
- **Never stitch separate answers into a claim the speaker never made.** Joining "the hospital" from one answer to "doesn't care" from another manufactures testimony. See [Performance Editing](../EDITING/Performance-Editing.md) for permissible compression.
- **Vulnerable subjects and minors are escalation cases.** Involve the producer and, where appropriate, a guardian or support person; reconsider whether the interview should happen; follow [Rights, Consent and Disclosure](../OPERATIONS/Rights-Consent-and-Disclosure.md).
- **Translation: literal versus faithful.** When an interview crosses languages, a literal translation can distort meaning (idioms, politeness forms, irony); a free translation can add meaning the speaker did not express. Use a competent interpreter or translator, record the original language cleanly, and where practical let the speaker (or a trusted bilingual person) review the translated quotes that carry the story. See [Localization](../TEXT-ACCESSIBILITY/Localization.md).

## 11. Worked example 1 — Founder interview for a brand film (hypothetical, Project B "Arc")

**Context:** 90-second founder-led brand film. The founder, Dev, is articulate and has pitched investors many times. The risk is not nervousness; it is a polished pitch that sounds like an advertisement.

**Listening plan:**
- Needed: the personal origin of the idea as a scene; one honest admission of difficulty; what Dev wants a user to feel; one specific design decision Dev cares about.
- Watch for: any moment where Dev drops the pitch voice.
- Off-limits: competitors; unverified health claims (legal).

**Conduct decisions:**
- Interview held in the workshop rather than the boardroom, among prototypes, so that Dev talks about objects rather than markets.
- Warm-up question about a prototype that failed — invites candour early.
- When Dev slips into pitch language ("we're disrupting the hydration category"), the interviewer does not interrupt; afterwards they ask, "What does that look like for one person on a Tuesday?"
- Patient silence after Dev describes their father's kidney stones — the reason Dev started thinking about hydration. Dev adds, quietly, "I never told investors that part." That line becomes the hinge of the film.

**Decision record:**

| Field | Entry |
|---|---|
| Intent | Viewers feel Arc comes from a real person's concern, not from a market analysis |
| Evidence | *Hypothetical:* the father story is told with hesitation and a lowered voice; the pitch material is fluent but generic |
| Constraint | 90 s; legal forbids health claims |
| Alternatives | (1) Build around the fluent pitch lines. (2) Build around the father story. (3) Use the father story but trim the hesitation. |
| Choice | (2), keeping the hesitation. It is the most credible moment and creates the emotional centre. |
| Trade-off | Must avoid implying Arc prevents kidney stones — a health claim. The film uses the story as motivation only and never connects it to a medical outcome. |
| Exception | If Dev or legal is uncomfortable with the family story after review, fall back to (1) with stronger visual storytelling; do not use the story without Dev's explicit approval after seeing the cut. |
| Validation | Dev reviews the assembled story section and confirms it represents what they meant; legal confirms no implied health claim; fresh viewers describe Dev as "genuine". |

**Capture:** A-cam at a 3/4 off-axis eye-line; B-cam wide showing the workshop; headroom planned for a 9:16 extraction of key lines; lav and boom; room tone recorded.

## 12. Worked example 2 — A nervous first-time subject (hypothetical, Project A "Second Shift")

**Context:** Asha has never been filmed. In the pre-interview she speaks freely; on the day she freezes, answering in single sentences and looking at the lens.

**Interventions:**
1. The director stops recording questions, chats for ten minutes about her commute, and shows her the monitor image. Asha laughs at her own framing; tension drops.
2. The director moves the interviewer chair closer to the lens and lower, to match her eye height; Asha now looks at a face, not a lens.
3. Questions move from evaluative ("What's the hardest part?") to concrete ("What's the first thing you do when you arrive?").
4. When she stumbles, the director says, "Take your time — we'll cut the pauses," and does not repeat the question.
5. Near the end, the director asks, "Is there anything I should have asked you?" Asha talks about her daughter asking why her mother sleeps during the day. She had not mentioned this in the pre-interview.

**Editorial consequence:** The daughter story — unplanned, specific and honest — becomes the opening of the film (see [Story Architecture](Story-Architecture.md) and the [documentary worked project](../WORKED-PROJECTS/01-Documentary-Second-Shift.md)). Because it involves her child, the producer confirms with Asha after the interview that she is comfortable with it being used and that the child will not be shown or named.

**What was preserved:** her 4-second pause before "I stopped noticing I was tired"; her self-correction "I mean — not stopped, I just — it becomes normal." Removing the self-correction would make the line cleaner and less true.

## 13. Counterexample and repair — the manufactured sound-bite

**Hypothetical counterexample.** A corporate social-responsibility film features a factory worker, Pritam, who took part in a training programme. The producer wants the line "This programme changed my life." Pritam's actual answers describe learning a useful skill and getting a small raise. Over four takes the interviewer asks: "Would you say it changed your life?" "Could you say that in a full sentence?" "Can you say it with a bit more feeling?" On take five Pritam says the line. In the edit it is placed after a slow push-in with swelling music. At the internal screening Pritam's colleagues laugh; Pritam later tells the HR manager, "I didn't really mean that."

**Diagnosis.**
- Leading questions supplied the content.
- Take-after-take pressure turned the subject into a performer of someone else's script.
- The edit then amplified the manufactured line with music and camera movement, compounding the distortion.
- The audience best placed to judge — Pritam's colleagues — detected it immediately. Credibility, the film's main asset, was lost.

**Repair.**
1. **Acknowledge** to Pritam that the line did not represent him, and ask whether he would do a short re-interview.
2. **Re-interview with open questions:** "What did you learn? Show me. What's different on a normal day now?" Pritam demonstrates the new skill at his machine and says, with a grin, "Now when it jams, they call me."
3. **Truthful assembly:** use the demonstration and his own line; drop the swelling cue in favour of the factory's real sound with a light musical bed only after his line (see [Music Selection](../AUDIO/Music-Selection.md)).
4. **Speaker review:** Pritam watches the new cut and approves it.
5. **Consent and disclosure check:** confirm the release covers the re-interview; record the review.

**Verification.** Show the new cut to a small group of colleagues; ask what Pritam gained from the programme. Success: they describe the skill and his new role without laughing at the film. The new line is smaller than "changed my life" and more persuasive because it is true.

## 14. Micro-decisions

- Sit at the subject's eye height; a standing interviewer creates a looking-up eye-line that reads as deference or submission.
- Remove the question sheet from your hands after the warm-up; hold it on your knee or memorise the listening plan.
- Ask one question at a time and stop talking.
- Repeat the subject's own words when following up ("You said 'normal' — what's normal?") rather than paraphrasing into your words.
- Let the camera roll through breaks if the subject consents; candid moments often occur then. Never use such moments in ways the subject would not expect without their agreement.
- Log the timecode of every answer the director thinks is a hero moment, live.
- Note on the log when a subject asks for something to be off the record; honour it, and tell the editor.

## 15. Failure signatures and corrections

| Symptom (in the edit) | Likely cause in the interview | Correction now | Prevention next time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Answers are generic and interchangeable | Abstract or evaluative questions | Look for scenes in the full takes; if none, consider a short follow-up interview | Ask for scenes; chronology before interpretation |
| Subject sounds rehearsed | Full-sentence demands; pitch-trained subject; too many retakes | Use the moments between formal answers; use reactions | Workshop setting; follow-ups on their own words |
| Answers cannot be cut together | Inconsistent eye-line, framing or sound | Use B-cam, cutaways, or let jumps show if honest | Fixed eye-line; two cameras; consistent mic position |
| Interviewer "mm-hmm" all over answers | Audible affirmations | Spectral repair where feasible; choose takes without overlaps | Silent nods; separate interviewer mic |
| Key line has no handle | Camera cut too early | Use the B-cam; use a cutaway | Roll before and after every answer |
| A powerful line raises consent concerns | Sensitive material not pre-agreed | Pause use; ask the subject; escalate | Agree sensitive areas in advance |

## 16. Verification before picture lock

1. **Selects against full takes:** for every interview line in the cut, open the full answer and confirm that the edited version does not change its meaning, omit a qualification that matters, or place it as an answer to a different question.
2. **Fresh-viewer comprehension of the subject's meaning:** ask a viewer who has not seen the raw footage to summarise what the subject believes. Compare with the full interview.
3. **Speaker review where practical:** let the subject see their sections, especially in sensitive or translated material. This is a review of representation, not an editorial veto unless agreed — the agreement should be clear in advance. How to run that round with care is covered in [Review and Revisions](../OPERATIONS/Review-and-Revisions.md#care-of-the-subject-who-sees-the-cut).
4. **Translation check:** a bilingual reviewer compares the original audio with subtitles for meaning, not word count.
5. **Consent and disclosure check before lock:** confirm releases cover every use, including social excerpts and translations; confirm any agreed off-limits material is absent. Owned by [Rights, Consent and Disclosure](../OPERATIONS/Rights-Consent-and-Disclosure.md).
