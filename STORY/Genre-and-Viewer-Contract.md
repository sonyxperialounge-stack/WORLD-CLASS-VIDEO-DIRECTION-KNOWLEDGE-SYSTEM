# Genre and the Viewer Contract

**Purpose:** Teach how each genre sets an implicit agreement with the viewer about truth, orientation, evidence and pace — and how to make structural, editorial and stylistic choices that honour (or deliberately and openly bend) that agreement.

**Applies to:** Education, documentary, brand film, narrative scene, comedy and music-driven pieces. Platform-specific behaviour (feeds, autoplay, aspect ratios) is owned by [Platform and Format Strategy](../DELIVERY/Platform-and-Format-Strategy.md).

**Prerequisites:** [Story Architecture](Story-Architecture.md).

---

## 1. What a viewer contract is

Every piece tells the viewer, usually in its first seconds, *what kind of thing this is*. That signal sets expectations about:

- **Truth status:** Is this real, reconstructed, staged, fictional, persuasive?
- **Orientation:** How much will I be told about who, where and why — and when?
- **Evidence burden:** What must I see or hear before I believe a claim?
- **Pace and density:** How much effort will this ask of me, and how often will I be rewarded?
- **Emotional permission:** Is it acceptable to be moved, amused, sold to?

Viewers forgive a great deal inside a contract and very little when it is broken. A documentary may be slow; it may not invent. An advertisement may be persuasive; it may not lie about the product. A comedy may be absurd; it may not be unfunny for long. The contract is why identical techniques — a slow push-in, a swelling cue, a jump cut — are honest in one genre and manipulative in another.

## 2. Contracts by genre

### 2.1 Education and explainers

| Dimension | Contract |
|---|---|
| Truth status | Accurate, checkable, scoped (what is *not* claimed matters) |
| Orientation | High and early: state the destination ("by the end you'll…") |
| Evidence burden | High: demonstrations, sources, data shown honestly |
| Pacing needs | Paced to comprehension, not excitement; pauses after new concepts; recap at transitions |
| Emotional permission | Curiosity and competence; mild humour; fear only when warranted and paired with action |
| Common breach | Sensational hooks that over-promise; animated "proof" with no source; speed that outruns understanding |

**Project C example:** the opening shows cloudy water and states the promise. The mechanism is shown with a cross-section animation *after* the real filter has been seen, so viewers connect the diagram to the object.

### 2.2 Documentary

| Dimension | Contract |
|---|---|
| Truth status | Events and testimony are real; any reconstruction, staging or synthetic element is disclosed |
| Orientation | Variable: observational documentaries can withhold orientation longer; investigative ones need clarity |
| Evidence burden | What is shown must support what is implied; editing must not create false causation |
| Pacing needs | Allows duration as meaning (waiting, fatigue, silence) |
| Emotional permission | Empathy, anger, grief — earned from the material, not imposed by music or editing tricks |
| Common breach | Misleading reorders, reactions from other moments, uncredited reconstruction, music that tells viewers what to feel about real people |

**Project A example:** the 4-second pause before "I stopped noticing I was tired" is kept because duration is evidence in this contract.

### 2.3 Brand film and advertisement

| Dimension | Contract |
|---|---|
| Truth status | Persuasive by nature; viewers expect selection and polish; product claims must be accurate and substantiated |
| Orientation | The product or brand must be identifiable; how early depends on format and placement |
| Evidence burden | Claims need visible proof (demonstration) or verifiable support; feelings need credible human presence |
| Pacing needs | Short formats demand a fast promise; longer brand films can breathe |
| Emotional permission | Desire, aspiration, humour, warmth; sentimentality is risky when not earned |
| Common breach | Demonstrations that are faked or exaggerated; testimonials that put words in people's mouths; implied claims (health, environment) that are not supported |

**Project B example:** the lid ring is shown advancing in real time on the actual product, with the app UI composited from the approved build — a disclosed technical substitution that does not misrepresent function. See [Compositing and Integration](../VFX/Compositing-and-Integration.md).

### 2.4 Narrative scene (fiction)

| Dimension | Contract |
|---|---|
| Truth status | Fictional; internally consistent; emotionally truthful |
| Orientation | Can be withheld for effect, but viewers need enough to follow relationships and stakes |
| Evidence burden | Behaviour must justify character change |
| Pacing needs | Driven by performance and subtext; silence and holds are tools |
| Emotional permission | Full range |
| Common breach | Characters acting out of character to serve plot; geography confusion that is not intended; music over-explaining emotion |

**Project D example:** the key reveal is held in silence because the contract allows the audience to sit with discomfort.

### 2.5 Comedy

| Dimension | Contract |
|---|---|
| Truth status | Fictional or exaggerated; truth is in recognition ("that's so true") |
| Orientation | Fast: the audience must understand the setup to get the joke |
| Evidence burden | Low for facts; high for internal logic of the joke |
| Pacing needs | Timing is everything: setups clean, pauses before payoffs, reactions given room; cutting too early or too late kills laughs |
| Emotional permission | Laughter, embarrassment, surprise |
| Common breach | Over-cutting reactions; music stepping on punchlines; explaining the joke with text |

**Hypothetical example:** a sketch in which an office worker pretends to understand a new coffee machine. The editor's key decisions are the hold on the worker's blank face (a few frames longer than comfortable) and the cut *to* the colleague's reaction rather than *away* from the worker. Comedy reaction timing is discussed in [Rhythm and Pacing](../EDITING/Rhythm-and-Pacing.md).

### 2.6 Music-driven pieces (music videos, montages, title sequences)

| Dimension | Contract |
|---|---|
| Truth status | Expressive; not literal |
| Orientation | Low; the music provides the structure |
| Evidence burden | Low for facts; high for rhythmic and visual coherence |
| Pacing needs | Musical phrasing governs; contrast against the music can be powerful |
| Emotional permission | Mood, energy, stylisation |
| Common breach | Mechanical beat-matching for the whole duration; images unrelated to the song's emotional arc |

## 3. Contracts are signalled, not declared

The opening seconds signal the contract through:

- **Image texture and camera behaviour:** handheld observational vs locked-off controlled vs stylised.
- **Sound:** ambient room tone vs scored opening vs a voice addressing the viewer.
- **Text:** a factual location title ("District Hospital, 21:40") signals documentary; a logo signals brand.
- **Performance:** people living vs people presenting.

If the opening signals documentary and the piece later reveals that scenes were staged without disclosure, viewers feel deceived even if each staged scene was harmless. If a brand film opens with documentary signals (handheld, real-seeming people), it borrows documentary trust — and must not abuse it; staged testimonials presented as spontaneous are a breach.

## 4. Retention tactics are not universal

Many online formats use tactics designed to hold attention: a cold open on the most dramatic moment, a spoken promise of a payoff ("wait until you see the end"), very frequent visual change, on-screen countdowns. These can work in some contracts and damage others.

| Tactic | Where it can serve | Where it damages | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cold open on the climax | Narrative and some documentary (flash-forward) | Explainers where the climax is a safety warning out of context | Viewers see the scary conclusion without the mechanism |
| Spoken promise of a payoff | Tutorials ("by the end you'll be able to…") | Brand films and documentaries if the payoff is a manufactured tease | Over-promising breaks trust when the payoff is modest |
| Very frequent visual change | Music-driven pieces, kinetic launch reels | Testimony; complex explanations | Change attracts attention but competes with comprehension |
| Curiosity gap ("you won't believe…") | Entertainment with genuine surprises | Factual and health content | Viewers sceptical of clickbait discount the information |

No single tactic is superior across audiences. Evidence that a tactic raised completion on one channel does not prove it will help a different audience, genre or objective — and completion is not the same as comprehension or trust (see [Creative Brief](../FOUNDATIONS/Creative-Brief.md#6-preference-versus-necessity-target-versus-vanity-metric)).

## 5. Worked example 1 — Same footage, two contracts (hypothetical)

A community kitchen runs a monthly free meal. A crew films one evening: volunteers cooking, an elder telling a story about the kitchen's founding, families eating, a heated argument over a burnt pot that resolves in laughter.

**Cut 1: Documentary short for a film festival.**
- Contract: real, observational, unhurried.
- Opening: the burnt pot argument, in real time, without music — it reveals the volunteers' relationships honestly.
- The elder's story is used whole, with its digressions, because how she tells it reveals who she is.
- No narrator; titles only for place and time.
- Ending: the empty hall being swept, with ambient sound.

**Cut 2: 60-second fundraising film for the kitchen's own social channel.**
- Contract: persuasive; the organisation speaks; feelings must be truthful but the purpose is an ask.
- Opening: a child's delighted face receiving food (with guardian consent), because the viewer needs to know quickly who benefits.
- The elder's story is compressed to its central line, checked against the full take so the meaning is unchanged.
- The burnt-pot argument is cut down to the laugh — its full tension does not serve the ask. Showing only the laugh does not misrepresent the evening.
- End card in the kitchen's voice with the donation link.
- A light music bed is acceptable here because the contract is openly persuasive, but it is chosen to support warmth rather than to manufacture tears.

**What is common:** neither cut fabricates. Both use real moments in honest context. **What differs:** orientation speed, compression, music, and who speaks.

**Verification:** festival programmers and fundraising viewers are different audiences; test each cut with its own audience. For both, confirm with the elder that her story's compression represents her meaning.

## 6. Worked example 2 — Timing under two contracts (hypothetical, Project D variant)

The same take of Ravi placing the key on the table is cut two ways in an editing exercise.

| | Drama cut (the project's actual contract) | Comedy cut (exercise: a parody version) |
|---|---|---|
| Hold before Nisha's reaction | Long; the audience studies her face as she understands | Short; cut to her reaction as the key lands |
| Reaction length | Extended; silence; refrigerator hum | Brief; a double-take, then cut |
| Music | None | A comic sting on the cut |
| What the viewer feels | Re-evaluation; sorrow | Surprise; laughter at the absurd formality of the reveal |

Same frames, different contracts. The exercise demonstrates why "the right length" for a reaction cannot be set without knowing the contract.

## 7. Counterexample and repair — imported tactics breach an explainer's contract

**Hypothetical counterexample (Project C, early draft).** To "improve retention", a producer requests a feed-style opening: a fast montage of dirty water, a red warning siren sound, the text "YOUR FILTER COULD BE MAKING YOU SICK", then a jump to the presenter saying "Stay till the end for the one mistake everyone makes."

In a small test with the target audience, two viewers stop watching because they assume it is an advert for a new filter; one viewer's comment is that "it's trying to scare me"; the remaining viewers recall the scary claim but not the steps. The public-health advisor objects that the opening claim is broader than the evidence.

**Diagnosis.** Three tactics from a different contract (curiosity gap, fear hook, payoff tease) were imported into an educational contract whose central requirement is trust and accuracy. They also misallocated time: 12 seconds of alarm left less time for the steps.

**Repair.**
1. Replace the montage with a calm, real image: water poured from a neglected filter next to water from a clean one, side by side.
2. Replace the alarm text with a scoped, sourced statement approved by the advisor ("A filter that isn't cleaned can stop working properly"), and a clear promise ("Here's how it works and how to clean it in five minutes").
3. Remove the payoff tease; instead, show the step list at the start so viewers can see what they will learn.
4. Keep urgency through clarity and pace, not alarm.

**Verification.** Repeat the audience test. Success: viewers identify the video as instructional and can list the steps; nobody reports feeling scared into it. Retention can be checked afterwards in channel analytics, interpreted cautiously.

## 8. Bending a contract openly

Contracts can be bent — that is often where originality lives — but bending should be legible.

| Bend | How to make it legible | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Documentary with reconstruction | Disclose in a title or narration; differentiate visually (texture, framing) | A historical event reconstructed with actors, titled as reconstruction |
| Advertisement with documentary aesthetics | Use real people, real usage; do not present staged testimonials as spontaneous | Real customers using the product, with consent and accurate representation |
| Explainer with narrative | Keep facts accurate; use a story as a frame, clearly | A family's water story frames the mechanism, with factual claims marked |
| Fiction with documentary style | The fiction is clear from context or credits | Mockumentary comedy |
| Drama with comic beats | Tonal shifts motivated by character | A grieving sibling laughing at an old memory |

Disclosure mechanics (titles, credits, labels for synthetic or reconstructed material) are owned by [Rights, Consent and Disclosure](../OPERATIONS/Rights-Consent-and-Disclosure.md).

## 9. Micro-decisions

- Choose the very first sound deliberately — room tone, music, or voice — because it signals the contract before the first image is understood.
- In documentary, prefer factual titles ("21:40") over evocative ones ("The Long Night") at the start; evocative titles can come later once trust exists.
- In advertising, show the product working before saying it works.
- In comedy, protect the frame after a punchline — a cut too soon steals the laugh.
- In explainers, show the real object before the diagram.

## 10. Failure signatures and corrections

| Symptom | Contract breach | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Viewers feel manipulated by a documentary | Imposed emotion; misleading order | Restore truthful order; reduce music; let material speak |
| Viewers think an explainer is an advert | Advertising signals (gloss, alarm, teasers) | Use instructional signals: plain light, real hands, clear steps |
| Brand film feels fake | Staged "real people"; over-polished testimony | Real usage; natural performance; fewer claims |
| Comedy falls flat | Timing breaks the setup-pause-payoff contract | Re-time holds and reactions; remove music that steps on lines |
| Drama feels flat | Music or cutting explains emotion the performance already carries | Remove the explanation; trust the hold |

## 11. Verification

- Ask fresh viewers after 10 seconds: "What kind of video is this?" Their answer reveals the contract the opening signals.
- After the full piece, ask whether anything felt misleading or manipulative.
- In factual genres, check every structural or stylistic choice against the truth-status row of the contract.
