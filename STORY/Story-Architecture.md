# Story Architecture: Information and Emotional Structure

**Purpose:** Teach how to design and diagnose the large-scale structure of a piece — what the viewer knows, expects, wants and feels at each moment — and how to restructure material without distorting facts or time.

**Applies to:** Every form: documentary, brand film, explainer, narrative scene, comedy, music-driven pieces. Genre-specific contracts are in [Genre and Viewer Contract](Genre-and-Viewer-Contract.md).

**Prerequisites:** [Creative Brief](../FOUNDATIONS/Creative-Brief.md), [Interview Craft](Interview-Craft.md) for testimony-driven work.

**Owns:** The beat map and the open-question ledger. Pacing within and across scenes is owned by [Rhythm and Pacing](../EDITING/Rhythm-and-Pacing.md), which builds its intensity map on top of the beat map here.

---

## 1. The viewer-state model

Structure is not the order of scenes; it is the sequence of **changes in the viewer's mind**. At any moment, a viewer:

- **Knows** some facts (and does not know others).
- **Expects** something to happen, be explained or be resolved.
- **Wants** something — an answer, a reunion, a demonstration, a joke's payoff.
- **Feels** a state — curious, tense, amused, moved, bored, confused.

A scene "works" when it changes one or more of these in the direction the brief needs, and when the viewer can follow the change. A scene "drags" when nothing changes, or when it repeats a change already made. A scene "confuses" when the viewer cannot tell what changed.

The editor's most useful tool is to annotate a cut with those four states at each beat. It is slower than watching, and it reveals problems watching hides.

## 2. Forms of structure — not everything is a three-act story

| Form | Organising principle | Typical shape | Fits | Fails when forced onto |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Narrative arc | A character wants something; obstacles; change | Setup → complication → escalation → climax → resolution | Drama, character documentary, founder stories | A procedure that has no protagonist conflict |
| Argument | A claim supported by evidence and answered objections | Question → claim → evidence → counter → conclusion | Essay films, advocacy, investigative pieces | An observational portrait (becomes preachy) |
| Demonstration | A process shown step by step | Problem → mechanism → steps → result → check | Explainers, tutorials, product demos | Emotional stories (flattens them into instructions) |
| Montage | Juxtaposition and rhythm; accumulation | Theme → variations → peak → release | Music-driven pieces, brand mood films, title sequences | Material that needs causal understanding |
| Conversation | An exchange between people whose relationship changes | Opening positions → exchanges → shift → new positions | Two-handers, interviews, debates | One-voice explanations |

Most real pieces combine forms. Project C "Clear Water" is a demonstration inside a short problem-solution argument. Project A "Second Shift" is a loose narrative arc (one night) carrying a portrait. Project D "The Spare Key" is a conversation with a reversal. The Arc 30-second spot is a compressed demonstration with a montage opening.

Choosing the dominant form is the first structural decision, because it tells you what counts as progress. In a demonstration, progress is understanding; in a narrative, it is change in a character's situation; in a montage, it is accumulation and contrast.

## 3. Structural functions

These terms describe jobs a moment can do. They are not slots that every piece must fill.

| Function | Job | Documentary example (A) | Brand example (B) | Explainer example (C) | Narrative example (D) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hook | Make the viewer want to keep watching by raising a question or offering a reward | Asha's daughter asking why she sleeps in the day | A hand reaching for a bottle, then hesitating | "One in five filters we tested was making water worse." (Only if the advisor confirms it; otherwise a visual of cloudy water in a filter) | Nisha finds the door unlocked |
| Setup | Give the information needed for later moments to land | The ward at night; who is on duty | The ordinary morning routine | What the filter is made of | The siblings' tension; the father's photo |
| Promise | Tell the viewer what kind of experience this is | Quiet, close, observational | Calm, precise product | "By the end you'll be able to clean it yourself" | A family argument with something hidden |
| Stakes | Why it matters; what could be lost | Patients depend on two nurses | Forgetting to drink; feeling unwell at 4 p.m. | Illness from badly maintained filters | Losing the flat, and each other |
| Evidence | Proof that supports the claim or the feeling | Observational footage of her work | The ring advancing as someone drinks | Demonstration and test results | Behaviour that reveals motive |
| Escalation | Intensifying stakes or complexity | A difficult patient at 3 a.m. | — (too short) | The step people get wrong | The argument gets personal |
| Reversal | A change that reframes what came before | "I stopped noticing I was tired" | The bottle "talks" back via the ring | "Cleaning with soap actually clogs it" | The spare key was given to Ravi, not Nisha |
| Payoff | Fulfilment of a setup or promise | Daughter's question answered indirectly by dawn shift change | Calm confidence; name and benefit | Viewer can list steps | Nisha understands why her father trusted Ravi |
| Release | Space for the viewer to absorb | Long shot of dawn; ambient sound | Logo hold | Summary checklist | Silence; refrigerator hum |

### When to withhold, when to tell

Withholding information creates curiosity when the viewer **knows that something is missing and believes it will be supplied**. It creates confusion when the viewer **does not know what is missing** or doubts it will ever arrive.

| Withholding works when | Withholding fails when |
|---|---|
| The question is clear ("Why does she sleep in the day?") | The viewer cannot name the question ("Who is this? Where are we?") |
| Partial information is given (we see the key but not whose it is) | Basic orientation is missing (we do not know the characters are siblings) |
| The answer arrives within the viewer's patience for this genre | The answer is delayed beyond the point of caring, or never arrives |
| Withholding serves the payoff | Withholding is a substitute for a weak payoff |

In instructional work, withholding is usually wrong: viewers need the destination before the journey ("Here's what you'll be able to do"). In drama and documentary, it is often essential.

## 4. The open-question ledger

Every hook, setup and promise opens a question in the viewer's mind. Every question must be either answered, consciously left open with meaning, or never opened. Unanswered questions that were not intended feel like plot holes or broken promises.

**Filled example (hypothetical, Project A "Second Shift" assembly, 25 fps):**

| Opened at | Question in viewer's mind | Intended? | Answered at | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 00:00:05 | Why does Asha sleep in the day? | Yes | 00:00:40 (night-shift title) | Answered early — consider whether the answer is too fast |
| 00:01:20 | What happened to the patient in bed 4? | No — a cough was audible and the shot lingered | Never | Problem: either answer briefly or reduce the shot's emphasis |
| 00:02:30 | Who is Sunita? | Yes | 00:03:10 | Answered |
| 00:04:12 | Does she regret this work? | Yes | Left open deliberately; partially addressed by her final line | Intended ambiguity — test with viewers |
| 00:05:20 | Will things change for night staff? | Opened by end card | Not answered; the end card asks the viewer to act | Intended — the call to action |

The ledger exposes the "bed 4 problem": an unplanned question created by an emphasis the editor did not notice. Fresh viewers asked about it in testing.

## 5. The beat map

The beat map is the editor's structural plan. Each row is a beat — a unit in which something changes for the viewer. For testimony-driven work the beat map is usually drafted as a paper edit — the selected transcript lines ordered into a story before any timeline work (see [Paper Edit and Assembly](../OPERATIONS/Paper-Edit-and-Assembly.md)).

| Column | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Beat | Short name |
| Purpose | Which structural function it serves |
| Information gained | What the viewer learns |
| Emotional movement | From what state to what state |
| Visual evidence | What the picture shows that supports the beat |
| Sound role | What sound contributes: voice, ambience, music, silence |
| Transition obligation | What the next beat must pick up, or what this beat must hand over |

### Filled beat map — Project A "Second Shift" (hypothetical; times illustrative at 25 fps)

| # | Beat | Purpose | Information gained | Emotional movement | Visual evidence | Sound role | Transition obligation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Daughter's question (0:00–0:35) | Hook | Asha has a child who notices her absence | Neutral → curious, tender | Black, then Asha's face in daylight interview | Her voice only, then room tone | Must answer "why day sleep?" soon |
| 2 | Arrival at night (0:35–1:05) | Setup / answer | She works nights; the hospital is under-lit and quiet | Curious → oriented | Exterior at dusk; corridor; her changing shoes | Generator hum; footsteps | Introduce the ward and its scale |
| 3 | The ward (1:05–1:50) | Setup, stakes | Two nurses, many patients | Oriented → concerned | Wide of ward; her checking charts | Fan; coughs (not emphasised) | Show her competence before difficulty |
| 4 | Small competences (1:50–2:40) | Evidence | She does many invisible tasks | Concerned → respectful | Hands on drip, notes, a joke with a patient (consented) | Ambient; her laugh | Lead to colleague |
| 5 | Sunita (2:40–3:20) | Evidence; relationship | She depends on a colleague | Respect → warmth | Two nurses sharing tea at 2 a.m. | Their quiet talk (subtitled) | Lead into difficulty |
| 6 | The 3 a.m. emergency (3:20–4:05) | Escalation | Something goes wrong; she handles it | Warmth → tension | Handheld follow; patient not identifiable | Raised ambience; her instructions | Release needed after |
| 7 | "I stopped noticing" (4:05–4:45) | Reversal | Her tiredness has become invisible to herself | Tension → realisation | Interview; protected 4-second pause | Room tone only | Lead toward dawn |
| 8 | Dawn handover (4:45–5:30) | Payoff | The night ends; someone takes over | Realisation → quiet respect | Light changing through window; handover | Birds begin; generator stops | End card follows |
| 9 | End card (5:30–6:00) | Release + organisational ask | Night staff need support; how to help | Respect → resolve | Text in organisation's voice | Room tone fades; no music or a sparse, late cue | — |

A beat map is useful when it can be read aloud as a story: *We meet her through her daughter's question. We see why: she works nights… Then we realise she has stopped noticing her own tiredness. The night ends; someone else takes over.* If the read-aloud version is boring or confusing, no amount of cutting craft will fix the piece.

## 6. Restructuring while preserving meaning

Editors restructure constantly. Real events are told in a different order; answers from minute 40 open the film; scenes are compressed. Restructuring is legitimate when it **preserves factual meaning and temporal honesty** for the viewer contract in force.

| Restructuring practice | Generally acceptable in documentary | Requires care | Not acceptable without disclosure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening with a later interview answer | Yes — interviews are understood as retrospective | If the answer refers to an event not yet shown, ensure viewers can place it | — |
| Reordering observational scenes | If order does not create a false causal implication | When the viewer will infer cause from sequence | Implying event B caused reaction A when A happened first |
| Compressing a long event | Yes, if the outcome and key turns remain | When duration itself is meaningful (waiting, exhaustion) | Shortening so much that the nature of the event changes |
| Combining two separate days into one "night" | Only if signposted or genuinely immaterial | — | Presenting as a single night when the story claims "one night" |
| Using a reaction from another moment | Rarely; only neutral listening shots | When the reaction implies a specific emotion | Placing tears from one topic under a different topic |
| Joining parts of different answers | Only when meaning is unchanged and the join is not misleading | Must be checked against full answers | Creating a statement the speaker did not make |

**Temporal honesty** means the viewer's inferences about time and cause are correct, or deliberately and visibly suspended (as with an opening flash-forward). The test `[Practice]`: *If the subject and an impartial witness watched this, would they say it happened this way?*

For interview extraction specifically, see [Performance Editing](../EDITING/Performance-Editing.md#interview-compression-what-is-permissible), which owns line-level rules for compressing answers.

## 7. Compression

Every rough cut is too long. The question is what to remove.

| Remove | Keep |
|---|---|
| Repetition of information the viewer already holds | Causal links ("because", "so", "then") the viewer needs |
| Setup for payoffs that were cut | Contextual anchors (who, where, when) the viewer needs to stay oriented |
| A second example that makes the same point | The *best* example, even if it is longer |
| Transitional material that only moves between locations | Breathing space after a high-intensity beat |
| Explanation that the picture already shows | Moments of silence that carry meaning (see [Perspective and Silence](../AUDIO/Perspective-and-Silence.md)) |

**Compression failure signature:** viewers say "it's fast but I didn't get it" or "it just ended." This usually means causality or breathing space was removed along with repetition. The repair is rarely to restore everything; it is to restore the *one* causal line or the *one* pause that was doing the work.

## 8. Negative space, inference, irony and reveals

- **Negative space** in structure is what is not shown or said, leaving the viewer to infer. Showing Asha's empty home bed in daylight, without comment, lets the viewer connect it to her daughter's question. Inference engages the viewer more than explanation, but only when the inference is reliable — fresh-viewer tests confirm this.
- **Dramatic irony** gives the viewer information a character lacks. In Project D, if the audience sees the spare key in Ravi's pocket before Nisha does, the argument becomes painful to watch rather than surprising. Structure decides whether the key is a surprise (withheld from both) or tension (shown to the audience first). Each is valid; they create different experiences.
- **Reveal ordering:** reveal what changes meaning last, and what enables understanding first. In Arc's spot, revealing the ring *before* the viewer has seen the forgetting-to-drink routine makes it a feature; revealing it *after* makes it a solution.
- **Urgency versus intensity:** urgency is about time pressure (a deadline, a ticking clock); intensity is about emotional magnitude. A scene can be intense and unhurried (Asha's reflective pause) or urgent and low-intensity (a countdown to a product launch). Confusing the two leads editors to cut intense moments fast, robbing them of weight.

### How setup changes the meaning of the same image

The same shot reads differently depending on what precedes it. Film-teaching often cites early experiments in juxtaposition (commonly called the Kuleshov effect); later replication studies suggest the effect is real but weaker and more context-dependent than folklore claims `[Heuristic]`. The practical lesson stands: **context steers interpretation**, which is precisely why it must be used honestly. A shot of Asha sitting still reads as exhaustion after the emergency, as contentment after the joke with a patient, and as loneliness after the daughter story. The editor chooses which meaning to support — and in documentary, must choose the one the material actually supports.

## 9. Two contrasting structures

### 9.1 Educational demonstration (hypothetical, Project C "Clear Water")

| Beat | Viewer knows | Expects | Wants | Feels |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Problem: cloudy water from a neglected filter | Filters exist; theirs may be dirty | An explanation | To know if theirs is safe | Concerned |
| Promise: "In five minutes you'll know how it works and how to clean it" | What the video will deliver | Steps | Practical competence | Reassured |
| Mechanism: how the candle traps particles | How filtering works | Why cleaning matters | Clarity | Interested |
| Mistake people make | That soap clogs it | The correct method | Correct steps | Surprised, then attentive |
| Steps 1–4 | Each step | Next step | To remember | Competent |
| Check: how to know it worked | Test signs | End | Confidence | Satisfied |
| Limits: when to boil water | What the filter cannot do | — | Safety | Responsible |

The structure **front-loads the destination**. The viewer's question is practical, not narrative. Withholding the steps for suspense would frustrate.

### 9.2 Character scene (hypothetical, Project D "The Spare Key")

| Beat | Viewer knows | Expects | Wants | Feels |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nisha lets herself in with her own key; Ravi is already there | They are siblings; something is tense | An argument | To know what it's about | Curious |
| The flat must be sold — Nisha's position | Her reasons | Ravi's objection | Fairness | Sympathetic to Nisha |
| Ravi resists, without saying why | He has a reason he hides | A reveal | To know his reason | Suspicious of Ravi |
| Escalation: Nisha accuses him of sentimentality | Their old resentments | Explosion | Resolution | Tense |
| Reversal: Ravi puts down the spare key their father gave him "for when he got sick" | Their father relied on Ravi secretly | Nisha's reaction | Reconciliation or rupture | Shock, re-evaluation |
| Release: Nisha sits; silence | Nisha's view has changed | A decision | — | Moved |

The structure **withholds the key fact**. The viewer's question is relational. Front-loading the key would remove the reversal and with it the scene's meaning.

**Lesson:** the same structural vocabulary produces opposite information strategies. Front-loading is right for the demonstration; withholding is right for the scene. Neither is "better pacing".

## 10. Rough-cut diagnostic for beautiful but shapeless material

Many rough cuts contain beautiful moments and no shape. The symptoms: reviewers say "lovely, but I'm not sure what it's about," or "it doesn't build." Use this diagnostic sequence before touching the timeline:

1. **Name the central question** in one sentence. If two people name different questions, the cut has none. *Example:* "What does it cost one person to keep a hospital running at night?"
2. **Write the causal chain** — "this happens, *therefore* this, *but* this, *therefore* this." If the honest connective between beats is "and then", the cut is a list.
3. **Mark redundant beats:** beats whose information gain and emotional movement duplicate another beat. Keep the stronger.
4. **Find missing stakes:** where does the viewer learn why it matters? If nowhere, the beautiful moments have nothing to be beautiful *about*.
5. **Check the payoff:** does the ending answer the central question or meaningfully leave it open? Is the ending earned by setup, or does it rely on music to signal "this is the end"?
6. **Card test:** write each beat on a card, lay them out, and try alternate orders on the table. Cheap restructuring before expensive re-editing.
7. **Re-cut the spine first**, ignoring polish. Restore polish after the shape works.

**Hypothetical application (Project B, Arc 90-second brand film, first assembly):** the assembly contains gorgeous workshop shots, Dev's father story, prototype failures and product macros, in roughly shooting order. Central question named by the editor: "Why did Dev make Arc?" Named by the brand lead: "Why should I buy Arc?" — two different questions. Resolution: the brand film answers the editor's question (motivation), and the 30-second spot answers the brand lead's question (benefit). With the question fixed, the causal chain becomes: *Dev's father became ill → Dev noticed how hard it is to notice your own habits → so Dev built something that notices for you → but the first prototypes were ugly and annoying → so Dev made it quiet → therefore Arc feels like a companion, not a coach.* Two product macros are cut as redundant; one prototype-failure scene is extended because it carries the "but".

## 11. Counterexample and repair — the misleading reorder

**Hypothetical counterexample (Project A).** In an early cut, the editor places Asha's tearful moment — which occurred while she talked about missing her daughter's school event — immediately after the 3 a.m. emergency, in which a patient's condition deteriorated. Cutaway footage covers the join. In review, everyone finds the sequence powerful. Viewers infer that Asha cried about the patient.

**Diagnosis.** The cut created a false causal inference. It did not alter Asha's words, but it placed her emotion under a different event. It also implied something about the patient's outcome that the film does not establish. The power of the sequence came precisely from the misattribution.

**Repair options:**

| Option | Truthfulness | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Remove the tears entirely | True, but loses an emotional moment that is genuinely hers | Weaker but honest |
| Restore the tears to the daughter section, where they belong | True | The daughter thread gains weight; the emergency must stand on its own |
| Keep the tears after the emergency but include the question audio that shows what she was talking about | True but awkward; structure breaks | Confusing |

**Choice:** restore the tears to the daughter thread, placing them just before the dawn handover so they link to the opening hook. The emergency now ends with her quiet, practical instruction to Sunita — less tearful, more professional, and closer to the brief's "respect rather than pity". The film loses one obvious emotional spike and gains a coherent through-line from opening to close.

**Verification.** Fresh viewers are asked: "What made Asha emotional?" Correct answer: her daughter. Asha reviews the sequence and confirms it represents her. The open-question ledger is updated: the emergency's outcome is either shown truthfully (if consented and appropriate) or the question is closed by her instruction ("Call his family").

## 12. Micro-decisions

- Write the central question on the edit timeline marker at the start of every sequence version.
- When reordering, write the original timecode of each moved element in a marker; the context check before lock depends on it.
- A beat that does not change knowledge, expectation, desire or feeling is a candidate for removal, even if it is beautiful.
- Place the first answer to the hook early enough to prevent frustration in the genre's terms (a documentary can hold longer than a feed video).
- Close unintended questions cheaply — a single line or a shot — rather than removing the material that opened them, if that material is otherwise valuable.

## 13. Failure signatures and corrections

| Symptom | Likely structural cause | Correction | Verification |
|---|---|---|---|
| "I didn't know what it was about until the end" | No early promise; central question unclear | Add or move a promise beat earlier | Fresh viewer states the topic at the 20% mark |
| "It drags in the middle" | Redundant beats; stakes not escalating | Remove duplicates; add an escalation or reversal | Intensity map shows variation ([Rhythm and Pacing](../EDITING/Rhythm-and-Pacing.md)) |
| "The ending came out of nowhere" | Payoff not set up | Plant the setup earlier | Viewers connect ending to an earlier moment |
| "What happened to…?" | Unintended open question | Close it or reduce emphasis | Ledger shows no unintended open items |
| "It feels manipulative" | Restructuring created emotions the material did not support | Restore truthful order; remove imposed emotional cues | Subject review; source-context check |

## 14. Verification

- Read the beat map aloud as a story; it must make sense without pictures.
- Run the open-question ledger against a fresh-viewer screening: ask viewers what questions they had and whether they were answered.
- For every restructured element in factual work, compare against the original context.
- Watch the whole piece at full speed after any structural change; structure is perceived in time, not on the timeline.
