# Shot Relationships and Transitions

**Purpose:** Teach how the outgoing and incoming images relate across a cut — position, direction, luminance, colour, depth, scale, velocity, energy and meaning — and when to use continuity, contrast or deliberate collision; then the full catalogue of transitions with their use conditions and failure cases.

**Applies to:** Every edit point, every transition, every sound bridge.

**Prerequisites:** [Cut Decisions](Cut-Decisions.md) (the timing of the cut).

**Related owners:** Screen direction, the action axis and eye trace are developed in [Continuity and Attention](Continuity-and-Attention.md); this chapter uses them as relationship variables. Split-edit dialogue technique is in [Dialogue and Voice](../AUDIO/Dialogue-and-Voice.md).

---

## 1. Two images, two jobs

Every cut joins an **outgoing image** and an **incoming image**. Each has a job:

- The **outgoing image's job** at the cut: release the viewer's attention at the right place and in the right state — eyes on a particular point, a question raised, a movement in progress, an emotion registered.
- The **incoming image's job** at the cut: catch that attention, orient the viewer quickly enough, and deliver new information or feeling.

A cut feels smooth when the incoming image catches attention where the outgoing image left it. It feels jarring when it catches attention somewhere unexpected. Neither is good or bad in itself. The question is whether the relationship serves the moment.

## 2. Relationship variables

| Variable | Continuity (smooth) | Contrast (noticeable, meaningful) | Collision (jarring, deliberate) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subject position in frame | Incoming focal point near where the outgoing one was | Focal point moves to the opposite side, motivated by a look or movement | Focal point jumps with no motivation, forcing a search |
| Eye trace | The eye lands on the new subject without searching | The eye is led across the frame by a gesture or graphic | The eye must search, creating unease |
| Screen direction | Movement continues in the same direction | Movement reverses to signal return, opposition or change | Direction scrambles to disorient |
| Luminance | Similar overall brightness and contrast | Dark to light (relief, revelation) or light to dark (threat, intimacy) | Extreme flash-like jump, used as a shock (check flash-risk QC; see [Quality Control and Export](../DELIVERY/Quality-Control-and-Export.md)) |
| Colour | Same palette and white balance | Warm to cool marks change of place, time or mood | Clashing palettes for rupture |
| Depth | Similar depth of field and layering | Shallow to deep reveals context; deep to shallow isolates | Abrupt depth change disorients |
| Shot scale | Progressive change (wide → medium → close) | Large jump (wide → extreme close) for emphasis | Repeated violent scale jumps |
| Velocity | Matched motion speed across the cut | Fast to still (or reverse) for stopping power | Speed mismatch that feels like a glitch |
| Energy | Similar activity level | Busy to quiet for release; quiet to busy for shock | Constant extremes |
| Emotional implication | Continuous mood | Juxtaposition that creates a third meaning | Tonal rupture |
| New information | Incremental | A reveal that recontextualises | Unrelated information that the viewer must reconcile |

### Thinking with the table

Pick the strategy per cut from the moment's purpose, then check every row for **accidental** contrast or collision. Many "something feels off" notes are an unintended collision in one row — for example, a luminance jump between two shots the editor thought of as continuous, or a focal point on the opposite side of frame from where the eye was.

## 3. Continuity, contrast and collision as strategies

### 3.1 Continuity

**Purpose:** keep the viewer inside the story, unaware of the edit. **Typical tools:** match on action, eyeline matches, consistent screen direction, progressive scale changes, similar luminance and colour. **Use when:** the content matters more than the form — most dialogue, demonstrations and testimony. **Risk:** continuity everywhere produces a smooth but flat piece where nothing is accented.

### 3.2 Contrast

**Purpose:** make the change itself meaningful — a time jump, a shift in perspective, a reversal. **Typical tools:** scale jumps, luminance or colour changes, direction reversals, still/moving juxtapositions. **Use when:** the story turns, a new section begins, or emphasis is needed. **Risk:** contrast without a reason reads as inconsistency.

### 3.3 Collision

**Purpose:** create shock, disorientation or a new meaning from juxtaposition. **Typical tools:** unmotivated cuts between unrelated images, harsh sound changes, broken direction. **Use when:** the brief or scene genuinely calls for rupture — a violent event, an intrusive memory, satire. **Risk:** if the audience does not perceive intent, it reads as error; in factual work, juxtaposition can imply connections the evidence does not support.

## 4. The transition catalogue

The timing-based approaches (cut before/on/after action, reaction-first, hold, ellipsis, jump cut) are defined in [Cut Decisions](Cut-Decisions.md#4-timing-relative-to-action). The table below covers relationship-based and optical transitions.

| Transition | Definition | Use conditions | Failure cases |
|---|---|---|---|
| Straight cut | Instant replacement | The default; most transitions should be cuts | — (its failures are timing and relationship failures) |
| Match cut | Cut between shots that share action, shape or idea, linking them in meaning | When the link is meaningful (a child's hand → the same hand as an adult) | Clever links without meaning feel like a trick |
| Graphic match | Cut between shots whose shapes, lines or composition align | Smooth transitions between places or times; visual rhyming in brand and music work | Overused, it becomes decorative; can imply false equivalence in factual work |
| Eyeline match | Character looks; cut to what they see | Establishes attention and point of view | Wrong eyeline direction or height breaks it |
| Dissolve | Gradual blend between shots | Time passage; memory; softness; linking related images | Overuse looks dated or indecisive; mid-dissolve images can be muddy; a dissolve in testimony can suggest continuity that did not exist |
| Fade to/from black | Gradual transition to or from black | Chapter endings; time gaps; emotional weight; endings | Too many fades make a piece feel like it keeps ending |
| Fade to/from white | Gradual transition through white | Flashes, memory, overexposure as idea | Easily kitsch; flash-risk if sudden |
| Wipe / push / graphic transitions | Visible geometric replacement | Playful, retro, branded or instructional contexts where the edit is part of the style | Distracting in serious material; "transition pack" look |
| Smash cut | Abrupt cut from intense to calm, or vice versa | Comedy, shock, waking from a dream | Overuse dulls it |
| Jump cut | Time removed within a continuous angle | Honest compression in interviews; energy | Accidental-looking in drama without established style |
| J-cut (sound leads) | Incoming sound starts before the incoming picture | Pulls the viewer forward; the next scene's voice or ambience anticipates the cut | Confusing if the viewer cannot place the sound; overuse makes every transition feel the same |
| L-cut (sound lags) | Outgoing sound continues over incoming picture | Lets a line or ambience linger; shows a reaction under a voice | Can blur who is speaking; smears scene boundaries |
| Sound bridge | A sound (music, effect, ambience) carries across a scene change | Links places or times; smooths a jump | A bridge implying a causal link that does not exist |
| Pre-lap dialogue | A line from the next scene is heard before the cut | Sets up the next scene's question; creates irony against the current image | Viewer attributes the line to the current scene's speaker |
| Audio-led transition | A change in sound triggers or motivates the cut (a door slam, a phone ring) | Makes cuts feel motivated | If the sound is unmotivated, it feels contrived |

### Split edits are not automatically better

J- and L-cuts are often taught as marks of a sophisticated edit. They are tools. They help when the viewer benefits from anticipation (J) or lingering (L). They hurt when:

- The viewer cannot tell who is speaking (an L-cut where the incoming shot also shows a person whose mouth is not moving, but who could plausibly be the speaker).
- A scene boundary needs to be crisp — a reversal, a punchline, a shock.
- Every transition in the piece is a split edit, which flattens the rhythm as surely as every cut being straight.

## 5. Shot-scale progression and the angle change

**Progressive scale** (wide → medium → close) orients the viewer, then brings them closer. It is the reliable default for unfamiliar spaces.

**Scale jumps** (wide → extreme close-up) create emphasis and surprise. They are powerful when the close-up's subject is clearly the most important thing in the wide shot; disorienting when the viewer cannot tell where the close-up is in the space.

**Same-subject cuts need a change.** Cutting between two shots of the same subject from similar angles and sizes looks like a jump. A common heuristic `[Heuristic]` is to change camera angle by roughly 30 degrees or more and/or to change shot size noticeably when cutting on the same subject. It is a guide, not a measurement: what matters is whether the change reads as a new viewpoint rather than a stutter.

## 6. Worked example 1 — Night to dawn (hypothetical, Project A "Second Shift", 25 fps)

**Moment:** Beat 7 ("I stopped noticing I was tired", interview, day) to Beat 8 (dawn handover in the ward). The outgoing image: Asha's face in soft window light, after her line and a pause. The incoming image: the ward's window turning pale blue, a colleague arriving.

| Option | Relationship | Effect | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Straight cut | Luminance contrast (warm interior day → cool blue dawn); scale contrast (close face → wide ward) | Crisp; the reversal stands alone, then a new section starts | Abruptness after a tender moment |
| B. 2-second dissolve | Blend of face and window | Suggests reverie; time passing | A dissolve between an interview and observational footage can imply she is remembering *this* dawn, which she was not |
| C. L-cut: room tone of the interview fades under the first second of dawn; birdsong in the ward begins 1 s before the cut (J) | Sound bridge with a straight picture cut | The pause after her line breathes; the dawn arrives as sound first, then picture | Needs clean birdsong in the ward recording; must not mask the end of her pause |
| D. Fade to black, fade up to dawn | Strong break | Marks an ending | Feels like the film is ending; there is more to come |

**Choice:** **C.** The straight picture cut keeps factual clarity (interview and observation are separate times); the sound transition provides the tenderness the dissolve would have supplied, without implying a false link. The birdsong arriving before the image gives the viewer a moment of anticipation — dawn is coming — that mirrors the relief of the shift ending.

**Micro-decisions:** start the birdsong on a breath at the end of Asha's pause, not during the pause itself; keep the interview room tone under the first frames of the ward to avoid a sudden ambience change; ensure the incoming wide shot's brightest area (the window) is on the same side as Asha's face was — the eye moves across less.

**Verification:** fresh viewers asked, "When was she saying that?" should answer "in an interview, separately" — confirming no false implication. Audio-only pass: does the dawn arrive gently?

## 7. Worked example 2 — Graphic match versus straight cut (hypothetical, Project B "Arc", 23.976 fps)

**Moment:** in the 90-second brand film, the founder Dev talks about the father's illness. The editor considers a graphic match: a close-up of a round hospital water jug lid (archive-style photo from Dev's family, licensed and approved) → a close-up of Arc's round lid ring.

| Option | Effect | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| A. Graphic match (circle to circle) | Visually elegant link from past to product | Implies that Arc is a medical answer to the father's illness — a health claim the legal review forbids; risks feeling manipulative |
| B. Straight cut to Dev in the workshop, then to Arc later | The story moves from motivation to action through the person | Less visually striking |
| C. Graphic match from the family photo to Dev's own hand sketching a circle on paper (an early design sketch) | Links memory to the act of designing, not to the product as cure | Needs the sketch footage; still a visual rhyme but with an honest implication |

**Choice:** **C** if the sketch footage exists (it does in this hypothetical — filmed in the workshop). The match rhymes memory with *making*, which is the film's real claim. **Exception:** if legal and Dev both consider A acceptable and the narration explicitly avoids any health implication, A could be revisited, but C is still more truthful to the story.

**Verification:** legal reviews the sequence for implied claims; fresh viewers asked "what does Arc do?" must not say "it helps with kidney problems".

## 8. Counterexample and repair — the transition pack

**Hypothetical counterexample.** A 3-minute corporate onboarding film uses a different animated transition between every section: zoom-blur, light-leak, page-turn, glitch, spin. The editor's intent was to "keep it dynamic". Feedback from new employees: "It's tiring." "I can't remember the policies." HR notes that the glitch transition before the safety section makes it look like an error.

**Diagnosis.**
- Transitions drew attention to themselves and away from content.
- No relationship logic: transitions were chosen for variety, not to express the relationship between sections.
- The glitch implied malfunction at the most important section — a semantic collision.
- Some transitions produced brief high-contrast flashes; a flash-risk QC check would be needed (see [Quality Control and Export](../DELIVERY/Quality-Control-and-Export.md)).

**Repair.**
1. Replace all transitions with straight cuts by default.
2. Use one consistent section transition: a short (about 12 frames at 25 fps) fade through the company's neutral background colour with a section title — it signals "new section" without spectacle.
3. Use sound to mark section changes: a soft, consistent tone, mixed low.
4. Remove the glitch entirely.

**Verification:** new employees watch the revised film; a five-question recall check on policies is compared with the old version. Success: improved recall and no complaints about fatigue. (Small samples; treat as directional.)

## 9. Micro-decisions

- In dissolves, check the midpoint frame: two busy images can become mud; consider dissolving through a simpler moment of one of them.
- Match luminance at cuts that should be invisible by checking the area where the eye is, not the average of the frame.
- For a J-cut, start the incoming sound on a natural boundary (a breath, a beat of ambience), not mid-word.
- In graphic matches, align the shapes within a few pixels at the cut frame; a near-miss reads as error.
- Avoid placing a cut where the outgoing shot ends with a camera move decelerating and the incoming starts with an opposite acceleration — the eye feels a jolt — unless intended.
- When cutting from a dark shot to a bright one, consider a few frames' delay before the bright shot's key action, so the viewer's eyes adapt.

## 10. Failure signatures and corrections

| Symptom | Likely relationship cause | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| "That cut jumps" | Same subject, similar angle and size | Change angle/size; use a cutaway; or commit to jump cuts |
| "I lost where I was looking" | Focal point moved unpredictably | Choose incoming frame or reframe so the subject is near the previous focal point |
| "It flashes" | Luminance jump | Match levels; delay key action; check flash risk |
| "The transition feels cheesy" | Decorative optical transition without purpose | Return to a cut; let sound do the work |
| "It implies something that isn't true" | Match cut or bridge creating false causation | Change the relationship; straight cut; clarify context |
| "I don't know who's talking" | L-cut over another face | Shorten the L; cut to the speaker earlier |

## 11. Verification

- Watch each important transition from at least 10 seconds before it, at full speed.
- Check the incoming frame's focal point against the outgoing one's (mark both on a still if needed).
- For factual work, ask of every match cut, dissolve and sound bridge: *does this relationship imply a connection the evidence supports?*
- Watch sections on the target device; transitions that work on a large monitor can be muddy on a phone.
