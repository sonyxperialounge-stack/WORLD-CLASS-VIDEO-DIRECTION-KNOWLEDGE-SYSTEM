# The Precise Cut: Where and When to Cut

**Purpose:** Teach the observable cues an expert inspects when choosing a cut point, how timing relative to action, thought and sound changes perception, how to compare candidate frames honestly, and when to break conventions deliberately.

**Applies to:** Every edit — dialogue, action, demonstration, montage, interview.

**Prerequisites:** [Story Architecture](../STORY/Story-Architecture.md) (what each beat must do); [Decision Method](../FOUNDATIONS/Decision-Method.md) (frame/ms conversions).

**Companion chapter:** [Shot Relationships](Shot-Relationships.md) covers how the outgoing and incoming images relate (position, direction, luminance, scale) and the catalogue of transition types. This chapter covers *the moment* of the cut. Pacing across scenes is in [Rhythm and Pacing](Rhythm-and-Pacing.md); performance selection in [Performance Editing](Performance-Editing.md).

## 1. What a cut decides

A cut is the instant when the editor decides that the viewer's attention should move. It answers three questions at once:

1. **When has the outgoing shot done its job?** The viewer has received what it offered — information, emotion, action, a beat of time.
2. **What should the viewer attend to next?** The incoming shot's job.
3. **How should the transition itself feel?** Invisible, noticeable, jarring, graceful.

Most of the craft is in the first question. Editors often say a cut "feels right" or "feels early". That feeling is not mysterious; it usually tracks whether the viewer's *thought* has completed, not whether the *motion* has. The viewer is following intention, meaning and attention. Motion is one cue among many.

## 2. Cues to inspect near a possible cut

The following cues can be observed in the material. No single cue decides; experts weigh several and check by playing the result.

| Cue | What to look for | What it often suggests | Failure if ignored |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intention completion | The character finishes deciding, saying or doing what they set out to do | The shot's job may be done; cutting just after completion feels natural | Cutting before completion feels like interruption |
| Breath | Inhalation before speaking; exhalation after a line; a held breath | An inhalation often signals an incoming line or decision — a natural place to cut *to* a listener; a held breath is tension worth staying with | Cutting through a breath that carries emotion |
| Eye movement | A look toward something offscreen; a change in gaze direction; eyes settling | The viewer wants to see what the character is looking at; a natural cut motivator | Cutting away before the look is registered, or showing the object too late |
| Blink | A blink often coincides with a shift of thought | Some editors use blinks as markers of thought transitions — a heuristic popularised in editing literature, not a law | Cutting mid-blink usually looks like a mistake unless intended |
| Thought transition | A visible change of mind — a pause, a shift in expression, a new topic | The point at which the viewer is ready for new information | Cutting during the shift loses it; cutting long after it drags |
| Gesture apex | The peak or turning point of a movement (a hand reaching its highest point, a door at maximum swing) | Cutting near the apex can hide the join in matched action; continuing motion carries the eye across | Cutting before the gesture is legible confuses; cutting after it settles can feel late |
| Change in attention | Something new enters frame, a sound draws the character's head | The viewer's attention is already moving; the cut can ride it | Fighting the viewer's attention (cutting to something they were not ready for) |
| Sound arrival | A line begins, an effect lands, music changes | Cuts aligned to sound events feel motivated | Picture and sound events slightly misaligned feel sloppy, unless deliberately offset |
| Suspense | Something is about to happen | Holding increases tension; cutting releases it | Cutting too early releases tension before it peaks |
| Information saturation | The viewer has had time to read the frame (scan faces, read text, understand the geography) | The shot can now yield | Cutting before saturation loses information; long after, boredom |
| Incomplete action | A movement or line is mid-way | Cutting here creates momentum, tension or discontinuity, depending on context | Accidental incomplete actions look like errors |

### How these cues combine

In practice the editor watches the shot at full speed several times and notices *when they want to see something else*. That "want" is the composite of the cues above. Then the editor checks the cues frame-by-frame to understand *why* — and to make sure the want is not just impatience from having watched the shot thirty times (see [Rhythm and Pacing](Rhythm-and-Pacing.md#creator-habituation)).

## 3. Why a cut can feel right without hitting the beat or the obvious movement

New editors are often taught to "cut on action" and "cut on the beat". Both are useful starting points, and both explain only part of why cuts work.

- **The viewer tracks meaning first.** If a character finishes a decision before finishing a gesture, the cut can come at the decision; the rest of the gesture is redundant. If the gesture completes but the thought has not, cutting on the gesture feels premature.
- **Music beats are one rhythm among several.** Speech, breath, camera movement and the internal rhythm of action all have their own phrasing. A cut that lands slightly before or after a musical beat but on a breath or a look may feel more organic than one exactly on the beat. See [Music Editing](../AUDIO/Music-Editing.md#5-beat-aligned-and-offbeat-cutting).
- **Anticipation matters.** The viewer's mind runs slightly ahead. A cut that arrives just as the viewer begins to wonder what is next feels right; one that arrives after they have wondered for a while feels late, even if it is "on" something.

## 4. Timing relative to action

| Approach | Definition | Use conditions | Failure cases |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cut before action | Cut to the next shot before the movement begins in it, so the viewer sees the action start | When the viewer needs to see the whole action (a demonstration step); when anticipation of the action is part of the meaning | Wastes time if the start is uninformative; can feel slow in fast sequences |
| Cut on action | Cut during the movement, continuing it in the next shot (match-on-action) | When continuity of movement should carry the eye across a change of angle; hides the cut | Mismatched speed or position across the cut becomes obvious; overuse makes every cut feel smooth and uniform |
| Cut after action | Let the action complete, hold briefly, then cut | When the result of the action matters more than the action (the key lands on the table — we need to see it lie there) | Long settles feel slow; in action sequences, loses momentum |
| Reaction first | Cut to the listener or observer before the event's full presentation | When the reaction *is* the information (how Nisha takes the news matters more than how Ravi says it) | Can feel coy or manipulative if overused; withholds information the viewer needs |
| Hold | Stay on one shot past the expected cut point | Builds discomfort, tension, contemplation; lets performance develop | If nothing develops, it becomes dead time |
| Ellipsis | Skip part of an action or time | Compression; the viewer infers the missing part | If the missing part contains necessary information, confusion |
| Jump cut | Cut within the same shot or angle, removing time | Compression in interviews (honest about editing); energy; disorientation | Accidental-looking; in drama can feel like an error unless the style is established |

## 5. Worked example 1 — A dialogue turn at the frame level (hypothetical, Project D "The Spare Key", 23.976 fps)

**Setup.** Ravi, in a medium single, says: "Dad gave it to me." The line ends at frame **F** (the last audible syllable decays at F; at 23.976 fps one frame ≈ 41.7 ms). The editor wants to cut to Nisha's medium single to show her reaction. Nisha's take has the following *hypothetical* timeline relative to the matched point in her shot (her shot was filmed with Ravi's line played off-camera, so her reaction is real):

| Nisha's shot frame (relative) | What happens |
|---|---|
| N0 | Nisha is looking at Ravi's face, neutral, still listening |
| N+4 | A small blink |
| N+10 | Her eyes drop to the key on the table |
| N+18 | Her mouth opens slightly; breath in |
| N+30 | She looks back up at Ravi |
| N+42 | She begins to speak: "When?" |

The editor is choosing two things: **where to leave Ravi** (relative to F) and **where to enter Nisha** (relative to N0). The table compares five candidates, all entering Nisha at the point aligned to Ravi's line ending unless stated.

| Candidate | Leave Ravi at | Enter Nisha at | Perceptual consequence | When it works |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | F − 6 (≈ 250 ms before the line ends) | N0, with Ravi's last word continuing over Nisha (L-cut) | We see Nisha hear the end of the line. Her stillness reads as shock before she has reacted | When the *hearing* is the event; puts emphasis on her vulnerability |
| B | F (exactly at line end) | N0 | Standard: line finishes, we cut to listener. Clean but slightly neutral | Default; useful when the scene's rhythm should not draw attention |
| C | F + 8 (≈ 333 ms of Ravi after the line) | N0 + 8 (so her blink is excluded) | We stay on Ravi long enough to see him look away after speaking — his guilt or vulnerability registers. Cutting to Nisha after her blink lands us directly in her eyes dropping to the key | When Ravi's after-line expression matters; makes the beat feel deliberate |
| D | F + 24 (≈ 1 s) | N0 + 24 | Held long on Ravi; we miss Nisha's eye drop (N+10) | Only if the scene is about Ravi's experience of speaking; otherwise we lose the key information |
| E | F − 18 (before he finishes) | N0, with the line continuing over her | We cut mid-sentence: "Dad gave—" then Nisha. Emphasises her pre-emption: she realises before he finishes | When the audience should feel Nisha already knows; risky if the line's meaning is not yet clear |

**Recommendation for this scene's intent** (the reversal should land as Nisha's re-evaluation, not Ravi's confession): **Candidate A or C**, decided by Ravi's performance after the line.

- If Ravi's after-line expression adds information (he looks away, ashamed), **C**: we see his vulnerability, then her realisation.
- If Ravi's after-line expression is neutral, **A**: the line's tail runs over Nisha, and her stillness carries the moment.

**Boundary conditions:** if the festival audience needs more time to register that the key is the father's spare key (an earlier setup was cut), B with a slightly longer hold on Nisha may be clearer. If the scene's director wants a more aggressive rhythm, E can work but only after the key's significance has been established earlier.

**Micro-decisions inside the choice:**
- Exclude Nisha's blink at N+4 if entering on her (a blink right after a cut can read as a jump).
- Keep Ravi's breath at the end of his line if audible; it is part of the confession.
- Do not let Nisha's "When?" start sooner than her look back up (N+30): speaking before the look would make her seem to be reading a line.

**A/B verification method:**
1. Duplicate the sequence; make candidate A in one and C in the other.
2. Watch each from **10 seconds before the cut** at full speed, sound on, on the primary display. Never judge a cut by starting playback at it.
3. Note which version makes you look at the key on the table. Note when you first understand that Nisha is re-evaluating.
4. Watch both again with sound off. The best cut usually survives silence; a cut that only works with sound may depend on the line's tail.
5. Show both, in random order, to someone who has not seen the scene; ask what Nisha is feeling. Their answers are evidence about clarity, not proof of audience-wide preference.

**Confidence:** the general principle — cut to the listener when the listener's reaction carries the new information — is `[Heuristic]`. The frame offsets are `[Illustrative]`; with real footage the right frame can only be found by watching it.

## 6. Worked example 2 — A product action sequence (hypothetical, Project B "Arc", 23.976 fps)

**Beat:** A hand twists Arc's lid open; the lid ring illuminates as the person drinks; the ring advances one segment. Shots available:

- **S1:** Medium, hand enters frame, grips lid, twists (twist lasts frames T0–T14).
- **S2:** Macro of the lid ring as the bottle tilts; the ring segment lights at frame R0 (from approved product footage), glow completes at R+10.
- **S3:** Wide, the person drinks at a desk, ring visible but small.

**Options for the S1 → S2 cut:**

| Candidate | Cut point | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| A | Cut on action: at T7 (mid-twist), continuing the twist in S2 | Smooth, continuous; the twist feels like one movement across two angles. Requires S2 to start mid-twist at matching speed |
| B | Cut after action: at T14 + 6 (twist complete, lid lifting) | Calmer; the twist reads fully; slightly slower |
| C | Cut before action in S2: S2 begins 8 frames before the ring lights | Lets the viewer settle on the ring and anticipate; the glow is the event |

**Recommendation:** **B into C** — let the twist complete in S1, then cut to S2 about 8 frames (≈ 333 ms) before the ring lights. The brief's stance is calm confidence. The ring lighting is the proof point; the viewer must be looking at the ring before it lights, not discovering it mid-glow. Cutting on action (A) would make the twist the star and leave the ring to arrive while the eye is still settling.

**Boundary condition:** in the 6-second bumper, time is too short for an 8-frame anticipation plus a complete twist; there, cut directly into S2 with the ring lighting 3–4 frames after the cut and remove the twist entirely.

**Micro-decisions:**
- Check that the macro's focus is on the ring segment that lights, not the adjacent one.
- Ensure the light's onset in picture aligns with the ring's sound event within a frame — or deliberately place the sound 1–2 frames early, which can make the light feel "caused" (see [Sound Design](../AUDIO/Sound-Design.md)).
- In the vertical version, the ring must be within the safe area at the moment it lights.

**Verification:** watch the sequence muted on a phone at arm's length (the primary viewing condition in the brief). Can you see the ring light? If not, the cut is not the problem — the shot scale is (see [Composition and Optics](../CAMERA/Composition-and-Optics.md)).

## 7. Dialogue editing: whose face, and when

### 7.1 The ping-pong problem

The most common dialogue-editing failure is cutting to whoever is speaking, line by line. It is "correct" — we always see the speaker — and usually dull, because it shows the least informative face. The speaker's face tells us what they mean; the listener's face tells us what it *does*.

| Choose the speaker when | Choose the listener when |
|---|---|
| The line's delivery carries subtext the words do not | The line lands on the listener — a revelation, an insult, a confession |
| The speaker is changing their mind as they speak | The listener's silence is the story |
| It is a monologue whose build matters | The audience needs to see how the information is received |

### 7.2 Overlaps

Real conversation overlaps. Edited conversations without overlaps can feel mechanical. Preserving or creating an overlap (the listener starts responding before the speaker finishes) can restore liveliness — but requires clean separated audio and careful dialogue editing (see [Dialogue and Voice](../AUDIO/Dialogue-and-Voice.md)). Do not create overlaps in documentary that change who interrupted whom.

## 8. Deliberate rule breaking

Conventions — match action, preserve screen direction, avoid jump cuts, cut away from long holds — exist because they usually make the edit invisible. Breaking them makes the edit visible. That is useful when visibility serves the brief.

| Break | Communication benefit | What the audience must still understand | Evidence that would make you undo it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Missing action (cutting out the middle of a movement) | Compression; energy; a sense of urgency or dislocation | Where the character ends up and why | Fresh viewers cannot say how the character got there, and the confusion is not intended |
| Mismatched screen direction | Disorientation; a character losing control; chaos in a fight | Who is who; the overall geography afterward | Viewers mistake one character for another |
| Harsh cut (sudden scale, luminance or sound change) | Impact; shock; tonal rupture | That the rupture is intended (often by context or sound) | It reads as an error or a technical glitch |
| Prolonged hold | Discomfort; contemplation; let performance develop | That nothing has gone wrong; something is still happening (even if it is just the face) | Viewers check their phones; nothing changes in the shot |
| Imperfect continuity for performance | The stronger performance outweighs the mismatch | The story and the emotion | The mismatch distracts at normal speed for a fresh viewer |

### The undo test

For every deliberate break, write one line: *I am breaking X because Y; I will undo it if Z.* Then test Z with someone who has not seen the cut. This prevents justifying accidents after the fact.

## 9. Technically correct continuity versus emotionally credible performance

**Hypothetical case (Project D).** In take 3 of Nisha's single, she holds the photo frame in her right hand throughout the confrontation. In take 5 — a much stronger, more broken performance of the key reveal — she has put the frame down on the table before the reveal. The master shot matches take 3.

| Option | Continuity | Performance | Viewer experience |
|---|---|---|---|
| Use take 3 throughout | Perfect | Competent, less raw | Smooth; the reveal is underplayed |
| Use take 5 for the reveal; cut away to Ravi when the frame changes hands | The frame "jumps" from hand to table across the cutaway | Strongest | The emotional peak lands; most viewers will not notice the frame because their attention is on her face and the key |
| Use take 5 and paint the frame back in (VFX) | Perfect | Strongest | Costly; unnecessary if the jump is unnoticed |

**Choice:** take 5 with a cutaway to Ravi. **Exception:** if the frame becomes a story element in the next beat (she picks it up again), the jump becomes noticeable and must be resolved — either a brief insert showing her putting it down, or the VFX fix.

**Verification:** a fresh viewer watches the scene at full speed. Ask afterward: "Did anything look wrong?" If they mention the frame, reconsider. If not, the performance choice stands. See [Continuity and Attention](Continuity-and-Attention.md) for the continuity matrix.

## 10. Counterexample and repair — the mechanical beat-synced product montage

**Hypothetical counterexample (Project B, first draft of the 30-second spot).** The editor cuts the morning-routine montage exactly on every beat of a 120 BPM track (a beat every 0.5 s ≈ 12 frames at 23.976 fps). Twenty-four consecutive cuts land precisely on beats. The product reveal also lands on a beat, preceded by the same rhythm.

**Reviewer notes:** "It's slick but it feels like a template." "The reveal doesn't feel special." "I can't remember any of the morning shots."

**Diagnosis.**
- **Predictability:** after a few cuts, viewers anticipate every cut; nothing surprises.
- **No hierarchy:** every shot gets the same duration, so nothing seems more important than anything else.
- **The reveal is not differentiated:** it has the same rhythmic weight as a shot of keys.
- **Action is cut off:** shots end on the beat regardless of whether their action has completed (a hand reaching for a coffee cup is cut before it grasps it), which creates a vague dissatisfaction.

**Repair:**

1. **Let action, not the grid, decide most cut points.** Each morning shot ends when its gesture completes; this pushes some cuts slightly off the beat — a frame or several — and gives the sequence a human feel.
2. **Vary shot durations** within the montage: short, short, longer, short, longest — the longest being the moment the person reaches for a bottle and hesitates.
3. **Create anticipation before the reveal:** drop the rhythm. Let the music phrase resolve; hold two beats of near-silence (room tone only) on the hand hovering over Arc; then reveal the ring lighting on a soft sound event, *not* on a downbeat.
4. **Keep a few cuts exactly on strong beats** where emphasis is wanted (the alarm clock, the door closing) so synchronisation becomes an accent rather than wallpaper.

**Verification:** compare the old and new versions at full speed with three fresh viewers. Ask which moment they remember; success is if the ring reveal is named. Ask whether anything felt "template-like". Re-check that the new rhythm still fits the 30-second duration. The music edit consequences (holding a phrase, creating the near-silence) are developed in [Music Editing](../AUDIO/Music-Editing.md).

## 11. Verification methods for cuts

| Method | What it reveals | What it cannot prove |
|---|---|---|
| Play from well before the cut at full speed | Whether the cut feels right in context | Whether it is right for other viewers |
| A/B duplicate sequences | Relative merits of candidates | Absolute correctness |
| Sound-off pass | Whether the visual logic holds | Sound-dependent intentions |
| Audio-only pass (picture covered) | Whether the dialogue rhythm works | Visual continuity |
| Frame-step through the cut | Mechanical errors: flash frames, blinks, mismatched positions | Whether it *feels* right — never judge feel by stepping |
| Fresh-viewer screening | Comprehension, attention, emotional reading | Audience-wide effect (samples are small) |
| Watch on the target device | Legibility at real size | Other devices |

A disciplined order: watch at full speed → identify discomfort → frame-step to diagnose → adjust → watch at full speed again from before the cut.

## 12. Micro-decisions

- Remove **flash frames** (one or two stray frames left from a previous edit) by frame-stepping every cut before a review.
- Avoid cutting on a blink in the incoming shot's first frames unless intended; it reads as a jump.
- When the outgoing shot ends on camera movement, consider whether the incoming shot should start with movement in a compatible direction or with stillness — see [Shot Relationships](Shot-Relationships.md).
- A cut that "feels a frame early" is often a sound problem: check whether the incoming sound arrives before the viewer is ready.
- Re-check cuts after music and sound design are added; sound changes the perceived cut point.
- When cutting interviews, prefer cut points on breaths or after a word ends, not mid-syllable (see [Dialogue and Voice](../AUDIO/Dialogue-and-Voice.md)).

## 13. Failure signatures and corrections

| Symptom | Likely cause | Correction | Verification |
|---|---|---|---|
| "The cut feels early" | Thought or action not complete; incoming sound arrives too soon | Extend the outgoing shot to completion; delay incoming sound with a J/L adjustment | Full-speed playback from 10 s before |
| "The cut feels late" | Information saturated; viewer already wanted the next shot | Trim the outgoing tail; check that nothing important happens in the trimmed frames | Fresh-viewer attention |
| "It's jumpy" | Mismatched positions, unintended jump cuts, cutting mid-blink | Choose different frames; cutaway; or commit to jump cuts as a style | Fresh viewer does not notice |
| "Everything feels the same" | Uniform shot lengths; beat-locked cutting | Vary duration by content; reserve exact sync for accents | Intensity map variation ([Rhythm and Pacing](Rhythm-and-Pacing.md)) |
| "I missed what happened" | Cut before information saturation; reaction-first withheld necessary information | Hold longer; reorder | Viewer can describe the event |
| "The emotion doesn't land" | Cut away from the face before the realisation | Stay on the listener; hold | Viewer describes the character's feeling |

## Confidence summary

Cutting on completed intention rather than completed motion, reaction-first for revelations, and the full-speed-from-before verification habit are `[Practice]` widely taught and used. The blink-as-thought-marker idea is a `[Heuristic]` from editing literature; useful, not universal. Specific frame offsets are `[Illustrative]` and must be found with real footage. The claim that uniform beat-synchronised cutting becomes predictable is a `[Heuristic]` with clear exceptions in music-driven work, where sustained synchronisation can be the point.
