# Performance Editing

**Purpose:** Teach how to select and shape performance — in fiction, testimony, presentations and comedy — based on intention, subtext, listening and truthfulness, and where the ethical limits of shaping lie.

**Applies to:** Any edit containing people: actors, interview subjects, presenters, comedians, founders.

**Prerequisites:** [Footage Assessment](../OPERATIONS/Footage-Assessment.md), [Cut Decisions](Cut-Decisions.md).

**Owns:** Line-level rules for compressing interview answers ([Interview compression: what is permissible](#interview-compression-what-is-permissible)). Interview conduct is in [Interview Craft](../STORY/Interview-Craft.md); sound-side dialogue editing in [Dialogue and Voice](../AUDIO/Dialogue-and-Voice.md); disclosure formalities in [Rights, Consent and Disclosure](../OPERATIONS/Rights-Consent-and-Disclosure.md).

## 1. What performance editing selects for

Editors choose performance for qualities that viewers feel but rarely name. Observable criteria:

| Quality | What to observe | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Intention | Does the person want something in this moment? Is it visible? | Intention makes behaviour readable; without it, lines are recited |
| Subtext | Is there a gap between what is said and what is meant, and does it show? | Subtext is where drama and humour live |
| Listening | Is the person actually hearing the other, reacting to new information? | Listening makes scenes feel alive; its absence makes them feel staged |
| Authenticity | Does it feel like behaviour rather than presentation? | Viewers detect performance-for-camera quickly |
| Vocal shape | Pitch movement, pace, breath, emphasis — does it match meaning? | A correct line with wrong emphasis changes meaning |
| Emotional continuity | Does the emotional state connect to the previous and next moment? | A great isolated line can break a sequence |

## 2. The best isolated line versus the best sequence

Editors often build a scene by picking the best reading of each line and stringing them together. The result can be worse than any single take: each line is good, but the emotional thread snaps between them because the takes were played at different intensities.

| Approach | Gain | Risk | Use when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Best line by line | Each moment polished | Emotional discontinuity; intensities jump | Short, functional exchanges; heavily scripted material |
| Best take as backbone, with selective substitutions | Emotional continuity | Some weaker moments | Most dramatic scenes |
| Best sequence of 2–4 lines per take | Balance | Joins must be hidden | Long scenes with uneven takes |

**Practice** `[Heuristic]`: first build the scene from the take with the most coherent emotional arc, even if some lines are weaker. Then substitute only where a stronger line does not break the thread — usually where a cutaway or a reaction gives a natural join.

## 3. Reactions: when they carry the scene and when they impose judgement

A reaction shot can be the most important image in a scene — or an editorial verdict the material does not support.

| Reaction carries the scene when | Reaction imposes judgement when |
|---|---|
| The listener's response is the new information (Nisha learning about the key) | A reaction from another moment is inserted to make a speaker seem foolish, dishonest or impressive |
| The viewer needs to see how information lands | An interviewer's nod is cut in after a controversial statement to imply agreement |
| The listener is the protagonist | Crowd reactions in a documentary are edited to imply a mood the room did not have |

In fiction, reactions can be taken from anywhere in the coverage — the actors performed for exactly this purpose. In documentary, a reaction placed after a statement implies that the person reacted *to that statement*. If they did not, the edit is misleading. Neutral listening shots (a person attentively listening, with no specific emotion) are generally acceptable as cutaways; specific emotional reactions are not portable.

## 4. Performance across genres

| Genre | What to prioritise | What to protect | Typical temptation to resist |
|---|---|---|---|
| Narrative acting | Intention, listening, subtext | The actor's arc across the scene | Line-by-line best reads that break the arc |
| Documentary/interview testimony | Truthfulness, specific memory, nuance | Hesitation, qualifications, the speaker's meaning | Removing every "um" and pause until the speaker sounds rehearsed; stitching answers |
| Presentations (presenters, explainers) | Clarity, credibility, energy suited to the content | Accuracy | Energy over clarity; jump-cutting every breath until the delivery is breathless |
| Comedy | Timing, the reaction, the beat before the punchline | The rhythm of setup and payoff | Trimming the pause that makes the joke |

### Presentations and jump cuts

Presenter pieces often remove every pause and breath with jump cuts. A moderate amount reads as energetic and honest about editing. Removing every breath makes speech feel relentless and harder to understand; comprehension relies partly on pauses at phrase boundaries `[Heuristic]`. Keep breaths at sentence boundaries; remove false starts and long hesitations.

## Interview compression: what is permissible

Interview answers are always compressed. The question is what kinds of compression preserve meaning. This section is the library's canonical rule-set.

| Compression | Permissible? | Condition | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Removing hesitations ("um", false starts) | Yes, in most contracts | Do not remove hesitations that carry meaning (reluctance, uncertainty about a fact) | "I — I think it was — maybe two years" → keep "maybe"; the uncertainty is factual |
| Removing digressions within an answer | Yes | The remaining statement must mean what the full answer meant | A side story about the bus route removed from an answer about arriving late |
| Joining two sentences from the same answer | Usually yes | The joined statement must be what the speaker said in that answer, in that sense; the join should not cross a qualification | "We were short-staffed. [digression] That night I was alone." → acceptable if "that night" refers to the same night |
| Joining sentences from different answers | Rarely | Only if they are on the same topic, the join does not change the meaning, and a reasonable speaker would agree; often better to cut between them visibly | Two separate mentions of Sunita joined — check context of each |
| Reordering sentences within an answer | Rarely | Must not change causality or emphasis in a misleading way | — |
| Creating a sentence from words in different sentences ("Frankenbites") | No, in factual work | Not acceptable, regardless of whether the result "sounds like" what they meant | "The hospital" + "doesn't care" from different answers |
| Removing a qualification | No, if the qualification changes meaning | "Mostly", "not always", "I think" often matter | "It's safe, if you clean it weekly" → cannot become "It's safe" |
| Placing an answer after a different question | No, unless the meaning is unchanged | Answers respond to specific questions; context changes meaning | An answer about "regrets" placed after a question about "the hospital" |

**Covering joins:** joins within an answer are often covered with cutaways or a B-camera angle. Covering a join is fine; covering a **misleading** join compounds the problem by hiding it. When a join is borderline, consider making it visible (a jump cut), which signals to the viewer that something was removed.

**Verification for every compressed answer:** open the full answer, read the transcript of the full answer, then watch the compressed version. Ask: *Would the speaker agree this is what they said?*

These rules apply from the first assembly onward, not only at picture lock: the earliest joins are drafted in the paper edit, where the full answers are in view and a borderline join is cheapest to repair (see [Paper Edit and Assembly](../OPERATIONS/Paper-Edit-and-Assembly.md)).

## 6. Rearranged, fabricated and AI-modified performance

| Manipulation | In fiction | In documentary / factual |
|---|---|---|
| Reordering scenes | Normal | Only if no false causal inference (see [Story Architecture](../STORY/Story-Architecture.md#6-restructuring-while-preserving-meaning)) |
| Using reactions from other moments | Normal | Only neutral listening; never specific emotional reactions |
| Time-remapping a performance (slowing a look) | Stylistic choice | Disclose or avoid if it changes the perceived emotion |
| AI lip-sync, face or voice modification | Requires performer consent and contract terms | Not acceptable as untouched reality; if used (e.g., to protect identity), disclose |
| Synthetic voice to "fix" a missing word | Requires performer consent | Creates speech the person did not produce at that moment; avoid; if unavoidable, disclose |
| Reconstruction with actors | Normal | Must be disclosed as reconstruction |

The principle `[Practice]`: in factual work, do not present rearranged answers, fabricated reactions or AI-modified performance as untouched reality. Where reconstruction or stylisation is used, disclosure is required — mechanics in [Rights, Consent and Disclosure](../OPERATIONS/Rights-Consent-and-Disclosure.md).

## 7. Worked example 1 — Two-person scene (hypothetical, Project D "The Spare Key", 23.976 fps)

**Coverage:** master wide (4 takes), Nisha single (5 takes), Ravi single (6 takes), two OTS shots (3 takes each), inserts.

**Performance assessment (hypothetical):**

| Take | Nisha | Ravi |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Controlled, angry, little vulnerability | Defensive, a little loud |
| 3 | Strong anger, holds photo throughout; reveal underplayed | Good restraint; hand-to-pocket business clear |
| 4 | Lost energy; flubbed line | Excellent listening in the first half; overplays the reveal |
| 5 | Raw, broken at the reveal; puts photo down early | Restrained; voice catches on "Dad gave it to me" |

**Backbone choice:** Nisha's take 5 for the second half (from the escalation onward), take 3 for the first half; Ravi's take 5 throughout except his first-half listening, where take 4 is better.

**Join points:** Nisha 3→5 at the moment she turns away to the box (a natural break; a cutaway to Ravi covers the photo-frame continuity issue discussed in [Cut Decisions](Cut-Decisions.md#9-technically-correct-continuity-versus-emotionally-credible-performance)). Ravi 4→5 at a cut to Nisha.

**Reactions:** Nisha's reaction to the key is from take 5 — a real reaction to Ravi's line as performed in that take. Ravi's reaction to her accusation ("You always kept the sentimental stuff") uses take 4's listening, which is stronger; in fiction this is legitimate.

**Rejected alternative:** using Nisha's take 3 for the reveal (better continuity, weaker performance). Rejected because the scene's purpose is her re-evaluation, which take 5 makes visible.

**Verification:** a fresh viewer watches; ask what each sibling wants, and whether any moment felt "acted". If a moment is flagged, try the alternative take there.

## 8. Worked example 2 — Interview cut with missing coverage (hypothetical, Project A)

**Problem:** Asha's answer about the 3 a.m. emergency runs 2 minutes 10 seconds. The edit needs about 40 seconds. Only the A-cam covered the second half (the phone B-cam's storage filled). There is no cutaway of her hands for that part of the interview.

**Assessment of the answer (hypothetical transcript, translated):**
> "It was maybe three. The man in bed 6 — his breathing changed. You learn to hear that. I called Sunita — she was on the other ward — and while she was coming I… [pause] I did what we do. [describes procedure, 30 s] … His son came at five. I don't know if he knew how close it was. [pause] Maybe it's better he doesn't."

**Compression options:**

| Option | Result | Truthfulness |
|---|---|---|
| A. Jump cuts within the A-cam, visible | Honest about the edit; energetic | High |
| B. Cover joins with observational footage of the ward at night (not of bed 6) | Smooth | Acceptable only if the footage is clearly generic ward footage and does not imply it shows this event; risky |
| C. Shorten by structure: use the opening ("his breathing changed. You learn to hear that.") and the ending ("I don't know if he knew how close it was… Maybe it's better he doesn't.") with the emergency observational footage in between | Strong; the observational footage shows a different night's emergency | Misleading — implies the footage is the bed-6 event |
| D. Use the opening and ending with a visible jump cut between them; then the observational sequence follows as its own scene | Honest; strong | High |

**Choice: D.** The two retained parts of the answer are joined with a visible jump cut — the viewer sees the edit. The observational emergency follows as a separate scene; a factual title is not needed because the film's structure makes clear it is observational. Asha's procedural description (30 s) is dropped; her competence is shown in the observational scene instead — picture carries what words described.

**Micro-decisions:** keep the pause before "Maybe it's better he doesn't" — it carries ambivalence. Keep "maybe" in "maybe three" — it is factual uncertainty.

**Verification:** compare the edited passage with the full answer: does it represent her meaning? Asha reviews the sequence. Fresh viewers are asked whether the observational emergency was the same night as her story; the correct answer is "not necessarily" — if they say "yes, definitely", the structure implies too much and needs a separator (a time title or a different transition).

## 9. Counterexample and repair — polishing the subject into a spokesperson

**Hypothetical counterexample.** In an educational film on farming, an agronomist interviewee says: "Drip irrigation can save a lot of water — in our trials, around a third, but it depends on the soil and the crop, and the upfront cost is a real problem for small farms." The edit uses: "Drip irrigation can save a lot of water — in our trials, around a third." Cut. The agronomist later complains to the producer that the edit "made me sound like a salesman."

**Diagnosis:** a qualification that changes meaning was removed. The retained sentence is literally accurate but no longer represents the speaker's position.

**Repair:** include "but it depends on the soil and the crop" (6 seconds). If time is short, cut elsewhere. If the cost issue is outside the film's scope, it may be omitted *only if* the remaining statement does not imply the opposite — here, "for small farms" was a significant part of their view; the producer consults the agronomist, who agrees to the shorter version that keeps the soil-and-crop qualification and is comfortable with the cost point being addressed in narration.

**Verification:** speaker review; comparison with full answer.

## 10. Micro-decisions

- Mark the take number and source TC of every performance substitution in a marker, for context checks and continuity tracking.
- When choosing between two takes of the same line, listen with eyes closed once; vocal intention reveals itself without the face.
- Keep a reaction slightly longer than you think on first pass; trim later with fresh eyes.
- In comedy, test reaction lengths in one-frame or two-frame steps; small changes matter.
- In interviews, cut on a breath *before* a sentence rather than mid-breath after, so the next sentence starts cleanly.
- Never remove a speaker's self-correction if the correction changes meaning ("I mean — not stopped…").

## 11. Failure signatures and corrections

| Symptom | Likely cause | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| Scene feels "acted" | Best-line selection broke emotional continuity; listening absent | Rebuild from the best-arc take; restore listening reactions |
| Interviewee sounds like a spokesperson | Removed qualifications and hesitations | Restore meaningful nuance |
| A reaction feels manipulative | Reaction from another moment imposes judgement | Use neutral listening shots or none |
| Presenter is exhausting | Every breath removed | Restore sentence-boundary breaths |
| Joke doesn't land | Reaction/pause timing | Re-time in small steps; test |
| Subject objects to the edit | Compression changed meaning | Compare to full answer; repair; review process |

## 12. Verification

- **Factual context check** against full answers for every compressed line (see the table in the interview compression section).
- **Emotional plausibility with sound:** watch the scene with final dialogue editing; performance perceived with rough audio can differ from the final.
- **Comprehension without prior familiarity:** a fresh viewer describes what each person wants or means.
- **Speaker review** where practical in factual work.
